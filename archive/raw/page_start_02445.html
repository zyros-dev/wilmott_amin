Once we have found the decorrelated \( \tilde{Y}\) as in previous post, we have the equation

\begin{align}\label{4.6.9}
 &\tilde{Y_p} \,=\, \, \sigma(X_p) \,\,W_p\,\\
 & \,=\, \, \left(\sum\limits_{n=0}^{N}\, dh_n\, H_n(Z_{X_p}) \right) \,\,W_p\, \\
& \,=\, \, \left(\sum\limits_{n=0}^{N}\, dh_n\, H_n(Z_{X_p}) \right) \,\, \left(\sum\limits_{m=1}^{M}\, \tilde{ch}_m\, H_m(Z_W) \right) \, \, \\ 
& \,=\, \, dh_0\, \left(\sum\limits_{m=1}^{M}\, \tilde{ch}_m\, H_m(Z_W) \right) \, +\,  \left(\sum\limits_{n=1}^{N}\, dh_n\, H_n(Z_{X_p}) \right) \,\, \left(\sum\limits_{m=1}^{M}\, \tilde{ch}_m\, H_m(Z_W) \right) \, \, 
 \end{align}

Explaining above equation, part of Y that is orthogonal to X, is described as a product of non-Gaussian zero mean noise and a function of X denoted as \(\sigma(X_p) \). It is supposed that we do not know the precise Z-series coefficients of  \(\sigma(X_p) \) that are given in \( \left(\sum\limits_{n=0}^{N}\, dh_n\, H_n(Z_{X_p}) \right)\)  even though we have complete knowledge of \(Z_{X_p}\) for every observation P since we are conditioning on X. 
In the previous post, copied from the paper, we have assumed that we know these coefficients and we have given a regression based method that calculates the coeffiicients of Hermite series of W when coefficients  \(dh_n\) of the hermite series of \(\sigma(X_p) \) are known. 
However in this new post today, we assume that we do not know \(dh_n\) with certainty and we also want to find \(dh_n\) from data. 
In order to do that we take a reasonable intelligent starting guess of \(dh_n\) that are hermit series coefficients of   \(\sigma(X_p) \)  and we do the regression to find the coefficients of Z-series of W. Now we calculate the R-squared \(R^2\) explained by the terms below in the least squares regression to calculate the coefficients of W.

\begin{equation}
 \,  \left(\sum\limits_{n=1}^{N}\, dh_n\, H_n(Z_{X_p}) \right) \,\, \left(\sum\limits_{m=1}^{M}\, \tilde{ch}_m\, H_m(Z_W) \right) \, \, 
 \end{equation}
In the above calculation, we have excluded the R-squared \(R^2\) explained by zeroth order term below
\begin{equation}
\, dh_0\, \left(\sum\limits_{m=1}^{M}\, \tilde{ch}_m\, H_m(Z_W) \right) \,
 \end{equation}

We continue to iterate over the coefficients \(dh_n\) that are hermit series coefficients of   \(\sigma(X_p) \) and continue to  re-do the regression and recalculate the coefficients of W and recalculate the R-squared associated with higher order hermite polynomials terms of \(\sigma(X_p) \) . Our true value of \(dh_n\)  would be the set of coefficients \(dh_n\) found from this iterative optimization that maximizes the R-squared associated with the terms
\begin{equation}
 \,  \left(\sum\limits_{n=1}^{N}\, dh_n\, H_n(Z_{X_p}) \right) \,\, \left(\sum\limits_{m=1}^{M}\, \tilde{ch}_m\, H_m(Z_W) \right) \, \, 
 \end{equation}

In practice, we might be fine with calculation of just three values (or third order expansion of \(\sigma(X_p) \)) given by  \(dh_1\), \(dh_2\) and \(dh_3\) or at most till fifth order of Hermite-series so this iterative optimization should not be very difficult.  Taking a very high order expansion of \(\sigma(X_p) \) would usually be unnecessary.

 \end{align}


Explaining above equation, part of Y that is orthogonal to X, is described as a product of non-Gaussian zero mean noise and a function of X denoted as \(\sigma(X_p) \). It is supposed that we do not know the precise Z-series coefficients of  \(\sigma(X_p) \) that are given in \( \left(\sum\limits_{n=0}^{N}\, dh_n\, H_n(Z_{X_p}) \right)\)  even though we have complete knowledge of \(Z_{X_p}\) for every observation P since we are conditioning on X. 

We take square of both sides of the above equation as


\begin{align}
&{\tilde{Y_p}}^2 \,=\, \, \left[ \left(\sum\limits_{n=0}^{N}\, dh_n\, H_n(Z_{X_p}) \right) \,\, \left(\sum\limits_{m=1}^{M}\, \tilde{ch}_m\, H_m(Z_W) \right) \, \, \right]^2  \, \\ 
& \,=\, \, \left[ \sum\limits_{n=0}^{N}\, dh_n\, H_n(Z_{X_p}) \right]^2 \,\, \left[\sum\limits_{m=1}^{M}\, \tilde{ch}_m\, H_m(Z_W) \right]^2 
\end{align}

We re-write both the summations in RHS of above equation that are being squared and notice after expanding them, the new coefficients would range from zero to 2N instead of 0 to N and from zero to 2M in other expression in the summations inside squares. We write second of these expansions as

\begin{equation}
\left[  \left(\sum\limits_{m=1}^{M}\, \tilde{ch}_m\, H_m(Z_W) \right)  \right]^2 \,=\, \sum\limits_{m=0}^{2M}\, \bar{ch}_m\, H_m(Z_{W})   \,  
\end{equation}

Particularly, we want friends to notice that first coefficient associated with zeroth hermite in above equation would be equal to expectation of the squared expression or second moment of the respective equation on LHS of above equation which means that 

\begin{equation}
\bar{ch}_0\, = E \left[ \left(\sum\limits_{m=1}^{M}\, \tilde{ch}_m \, H_m(Z_{W}) \right)^2  \,\right] \,
\end{equation}

So we can re-write one of the previous equations as
\begin{align}
&{\tilde{Y_p}}^2 \,=\, \, \left[ \sum\limits_{n=0}^{N}\, dh_n\, H_n(Z_{X_p}) \right]^2 \,\, \left[\sum\limits_{m=1}^{M}\, \tilde{ch}_m\, H_m(Z_W) \right]^2 \\
&\,=\, \left[ \, \sum\limits_{n=0}^{2N}\, \bar{dh}_n\, H_n(Z_{X_p})   \,  \right]  \, \left[\, \sum\limits_{m=0}^{2M}\, \bar{ch}_m\, H_m(Z_{W})   \,\right]\\
&\,=\, \left[ \, \sum\limits_{n=0}^{2N}\, \bar{dh}_n\, H_n(Z_{X_p})   \,  \right]  \, \left[\, \bar{ch}_0\, + \, \sum\limits_{m=1}^{2M}\, \bar{ch}_m\, H_m(Z_{W})   \,\right]\\
&\,=\, \bar{ch}_0\,\left[ \, \sum\limits_{n=0}^{2N}\, \bar{dh}_n\, H_n(Z_{X_p})   \,  \right]  \,+\, \,\left[ \, \sum\limits_{n=0}^{2N}\, \bar{dh}_n\, H_n(Z_{X_p})   \,  \right] \, \,\left[ \, \sum\limits_{m=1}^{2M}\, \bar{ch}_m\, H_m(Z_{W})   \,\right]
\end{align}

We make the observation that \( \bar{ch}_0\, \) which is equal to expectation of square of W (second moment of W) cannot be zero. We also notice that since \(Z_W\)  and \(Z_{X_p}\) are both independent, the second term has a zero expectation because  \(\left[ \, \sum\limits_{m=1}^{2M}\, \bar{ch}_m\, H_m(Z_{W})   \,\right]\) is all composed of higher hermites each of which has a zero expectation.

We also notice that there are non-Zero correlations between  first 2N Hermite polynomials, \(H_n(Z_{X_p})\)  and the first part of above equation given as \(\, \bar{ch}_0\,\left[ \, \sum\limits_{n=0}^{2N}\, \bar{dh}_n\, H_n(Z_{X_p})   \,  \right]  \, \) and we can find this correlated part by regressing  \({\tilde{Y_p}}^2 \,\) which are squares of data values of orthogonal part (orthogonal to Y|X). If we regress the  \({\tilde{Y_p}}^2 \,\)  data values on first 2N Hermite polynomials of \(Z_{X_p}\) we will be able to find the coefficients \(\bar{dh}_n\,\) in Hermite series expression of   (these coefficients would be a constant multiple of true values of coefficients since there is a multiplication by \(\bar{ch}_0\, \) )
\begin{equation}
 \, \sum\limits_{n=0}^{2N}\, \bar{dh}_n\, H_n(Z_{X_p})   \, 
\end{equation}
 and that can lead to coefficients of \(\sum\limits_{n=0}^{N}\, dh_n\, H_n(Z_{X_p}) \) that we wanted to find since 
\begin{equation}
 \, \sum\limits_{n=0}^{N}\, dh_n\, H_n(Z_{X_p})   \,   \,=\, \sqrt{\left[ \, \sum\limits_{n=0}^{2N}\, \bar{dh}_n\, H_n(Z_{X_p})   \,  \right]}  \
\end{equation}

and we can find this square root on right hand side of above equation using our Taylor series expansion formula. However, higher coefficients after 3rd or 4th coefficients would be rather approximate due to noise in regressions but I am sure they would be very informative.

It seems interesting and we realize that a lot of analytics can be done by regressing the square, cube and fourth power of data values on 2N, 3N and 4N hermites.

\begin{equation}

Friends, in the context of previous few posts, we know that we could represent conditional distribution of Y as

\begin{align}\label{4.6.11}
 & Y\, | \, X=\, E\left[Y|X \right]+\,\, \sigma(X(Z_{X_p})) \, W  \\
 &=\mu_Y+\,\sum\limits_{n=1}^{N}\,\rho_{XY,n} \, ch_n \, H_n(Z_X)+\,\, \left[ \sum\limits_{n=0}^{N}\, dh_n \, H_n(Z_{X_p}) \right] \, \left[\sum\limits_{m=1}^{M}\, \tilde{ch}_m\, H_m(Z_W)\right] 
 \end{align}
where

\begin{equation}
 \sigma(X(Z_{X_p}))\,=\,\sum\limits_{n=0}^{N}\, dh_n \, H_n(Z_X) 
\end{equation}

but we know in the context of SDEs that usually
\begin{equation}
 \sigma(X)\,=\,X^{\gamma}
\end{equation}

meaning that function, that appears as a multiplier of zero mean noise W, is usually a power of the SDE variable at an earlier step. Sometimes, in stochastic volatility setting, we have two functions, one of asset variable and other of volatility, both of which appear as a multiplier of noise W. This stochastic volatility setting can usually be written as

\begin{equation}
 \sigma(X,V)\,=\,X^{\gamma_X}\,\, V^{\gamma_V}
\end{equation}

and our equation of the conditional expectation is given as
\begin{align}
 & Y\, | \, (X ,  V)=\, E\left[Y|\left(X,V\right) \right]+\,\, \sigma(X(Z_{X_p}),V(Z_{V_p})) \, W  \\
 &=\mu_Y\,+\,\sum\limits_{n=1}^{N}\, \boldsymbol{{x_n}^T \, Var_{{X},n}^{-1} \,cov_{\boldsymbol{XY},n} }\,+\,\, \left[ \sum\limits_{n=0}^{N}\, dh_n \, H_n(Z_{X_p}) \right] \, \, \left[ \sum\limits_{k=0}^{K}\, gh_n \, H_n(Z_{X_v}) \right] \, \left[\sum\limits_{m=1}^{M}\, \tilde{ch}_m\, H_m(Z_W)\right] 
 \end{align}

I made another observation that R_squared was not changing smoothly as I changed the function of X (or volatility) in the volatility term. 

So there are two important observations. First is that usually there is just one parameter of importance when we encounter SDE like evolution of uncertainty and this parameter is the power of SDE variable, somthing we have represented as gamma in above equations. Second observation was that R-squared did not change smoothly as we changed gamma. We also notice that gamma is usually between zero and one most of the times in applications of SDEs. 

So I made a grid of gamma with hundred grid points ranging from .01, .02, .03, ...,1.0   . I calculated R-squared on this grid and chose the value of gamma where R-squared was maximum. I also noticed that in close neighborhood of maximum R_squared (R-Squared>.995) , R-Squared changes very smoothly but after R_squared  decrease below .9, it can change very abruptly and can all of a sudden go to -25 and then go to .7 and something like that. But good thing was that close to maximum R-squared on the grid, R-squared changed extremely smoothly for many many entries. 

So this way I was able to make a grid for gamma and then find the value of gamma associated with maximum R-squared. I also noticed that several times, there were two local maxima for R-squared one of which would have gamma smaller than .5 while another local maxima would take place somewhere when gamma was greater than .5
 
Then I made a two dimensional grid of 40X 40 =1600 values of gammaX and gammaV for and changed  each of them by .025. And again this also worked very well and I was able to find a good two dimensional maximum for R-squared where all densities were very good and smooth and density of noise was also very smooth. I simply picked the value of gammaX and gammaV where R-squared was maximized on two dimensional grid.

Due to multi-modal values of R-squared, it would be important for friends to choose a value of maximum R-squared that theoretically seem as better fit based on analytics and experience.

But many times there would be several parameters instead of just one parameter associated with a function. And it could be possible to do a large spaced grid optimization for function parameters to four or possibly five dimension but not beyond that.

Friends, logic of the above program is very simple.
First I do an iterative optimization of third and fourth moment by perturbing the coefficients c2 and c3 in the direction of decreasing objective function. We first perturb c2 in one direction and then divide all the first four coefficients by resulting second moment to make sure that second moment of standardized moments remains one. We also alter co to make sure that mean remains zero. By division with second moment and adjusting c0, we make sure that once c3 is perturbed, first two moments remain calibrated. And only after above two adjustments, that we evaluate the objective function for change in c2 and accept the change if objective function decreases after all of the above changes. similarly for perturbations in c3.
Even if there is any slight slack in calibration of first four coefficients, we are extremely close to true values and a few iterations of multidimensional Newton root search fixes any defect.
Then we perturb c4 and c5 starting from zero on top of earlier four dimensional calibration. Please note that our perturbation step size is very small. After perturbing c4, we make a few iterations of multidimensional root search on first four coefficients while taking c4 and c5 as given. Even though c4 and c5 are included in all moment calculations at their given values, only first four coefficients are updated by multidimensional Newton to keep satisfying first four moments hence keeping them calibrated. This multidimensional Newton almost always works since after small perturbation in c4 or c5, we are still in very close neighbourhood of earlier solution and still firmly in catchment of Newton method. This is also computationally very cheap. We evaluate objective function to accept the move in c4 only after multidimensional Newton update on first four coefficients. And updates in the  values of first four coefficients are also included in calculation of the objective function. We accept the move in c4 if objective function decreases after all of the above changes. Similarly for c5.
We do a few passes of six dimensional Newton method to make sure if there is any slight slack in calibration, it is improved.
Then we perturb c6 and c7 that were originally zero to values away from zero. It is just analogous to what we did for c4 and c5. After perturbing c6 by a small step, we use multidimensional Newton method to keep first six moments calibrated. In these Newton calculations of first six derivatives, c6 and c7 are taken as constants and only first six coefficients c0-c5 are updated making sure that first six moments remain calibrated despite small move in c6 or c7. And then we evaluate the objective function that includes change in c6 or c7 and all the newton updated values of c0-c5. And if objective function improves, we accept the perturbation change in c6 or c7, 
At the end, We do a few passes of eight dimensional Newton method to make sure if there is any slight slack in calibration, it is improved.
I did optimization on pairs of coefficients since it might have been difficult to fit skew without fitting kurtosis while keeping variance unity and similar of all pairs of odd and even moments.
