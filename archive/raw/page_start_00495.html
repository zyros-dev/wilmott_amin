Sorry amike, I did not try to find the solution for the equations you wrote but I understand what you are saying and will try to propose a solution. There are a few considerations, however
Most transcendental functions are defined by series expansions. We are used to them and it is easier to use them since our numerical software knows how to calculate the series appropriately and where to truncate the series given the parameters or the domain.
Once we have calculated the resulting solution of an ODE, we can write an appropriate code that would calculate the series just like it is done for exponentials, or logarithms or trignometric functions. May be there could be intelligent software that takes as input first few terms of a series and then finds out rest of the terms given the pattern in the first few terms of a series expansion. People would like to give new names to functions defined by series solutions of interesting ODEs and then there could be laws how to differentiate(take derivatives) them just like we have for exponentials and trignometric functions which are essentially defined by series. 
Even if you want to numerically integrate an ODE, I believe you could take a larger step using a few terms of the series as compared to the step when integrating using Runge-Kutta and you would still have the same accuracy and better speed. I doubt that any numerical method could easily be faster than a solution method that works by finding the actual appropriate analytical solution even though that solution is given by a series.

Since the original purpose of this thread was to solve the SDEs with the method of iterated integrals, I want to write a few comments on the work I posted on this thread earlier. I had proposed a numerical method as in post 105 (It has small error in quadratic variation term if I correctly recall). The idea of the method was to solve the probability distribution of SDEs by dividing the initial density along the standard deviations of the normal density into small subdivisions. The value of normal standard deviation units at the center of corresponding subdivision on normal density was used to evolve the center of the subdivision for the distribution of SDEs. Later, the density of the SDE was constructed by doing an appropriate change of variable between the density of normal and the density of SDE since probability mass in corresponding subdivisions of normal density and SDE density must be the same. Here are some observations.
1. SDEs can be evolved like ODEs but for particular value of corresponding standard deviation units on the normal density. We have to use the iterated dz and dz, dt integrals using formulas I gave earlier in this thread.
2. I believe that accuracy using the method suggested in post 105(after accounting for small error on dt term) and later posts would  be because I was taking the value of corresponding normal standard deviation units at the center of the subdivision but the probability mass is defined by an integral over the subdivision. It would be far more accurate to see how the ends of the SDE subdivisions are expanding/contracting and then calculate the probability mass using change of variable. An analogy would be how accuracy changes when we numerically integrate using the single value at the center of the integration grid versus when we integrate using the values of function at both ends. I was using the mid-point version but we could just as easily use the ends and have more accuracy.
