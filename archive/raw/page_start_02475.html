Friends, even outside of stochastics, there could be quite a few very interesting applications of new Math AI. For suitable multivariate functions that we can represent as Taylor series without great difficulty, it would be possible to represent Taylor coefficients as Legendre series expansions in various parameters by regressing suitably large sample of coefficients of multivariate Taylor series over randomized data of parameters. Not only would we be able to find coefficients of Taylor series of  a particular function, We could also easily find multiple derivatives/cross derivatives of coefficients of Taylor series with respect to all different parameters. And we could also convert these coefficients of Taylor series of a function to its further functions (composition of functions) through Taylor series. So once we have found coefficients of Taylor series of \(f(X)\) as a function its parameters(and their derivatives with respect to parameters) through Legendre series we could easily find coefficients of \(g(f(X))\) and its derivatives with respect to parameters by composition of two series using Taylor series of \(g(f(X))\) where coefficients of \(f(X)\) have been found through our Legendre regression.

And this fact can also be used to make it easier to apply Legendre Series regression more accurately. For example, in SDEs we know that we could transform an SDE in original coordinates to another SDE in Bessel coordinates. So one could possibly randomize over input parameters and regress them on coefficients of Hermite series of SDEs in Bessel coordinates and once coefficients of SDE in Bessel series have been retrieved, we could easily convert hem to coefficients of Hermite series of SDEs in original coordinates. And we could also do these transformations for derivatives of coefficients of hermite series with respect to parameters of the SDE. 

And we could use these functional transformation in a straightforward way when dealing with non-random functions. If a difficult function could be converted into a little easier function (for example by taking square root or its log), we could find coefficients of Taylor series of easier function(that was possibly square root or log or something else) in terms of Legendre polynomials of the parameters and then convert these coefficients to coefficients of original more difficult function through composition of series \(g(f(X))\). And derivatives of coefficients of Taylor series (of easier function) with respect to parameters could also be converted to derivatives of coefficients of Taylor series of original (difficult) function.

Once we are more comfortable with numerical complexities of above ideas, I am sure we can move on to more interesting things. For example, once we observe a random variable or a function, we may try to know distribution of parameters that likely caused that particular observation(given for example some subset of parameters that we already know somehow in advance) . This might not be doable right away but I hope soon we would be doing many such interesting things with more research.

These are all possibilities but mathematics many times  requires precision and it might take us some reasonable time to understand all the problems that arise when dealing with these new ideas and try to find their solution so that we have good and established methods everyone could use for Math based AI.

Friends, I have been trying to get a good fit to SDE densities using regression on Hermite series coefficients on Legendre polynomials of parameters of the SDE. But one thing I noticed that there was a lot of noise in monte carlo of many SDEs especially when they were close to zero and volatility exponent was close to .5. And higher order SDE discretizations made SDEs even more unstable close to zero. I noticed that until problem at zero is properly fixed, SDE Hermite series coefficients with some parameters would continue to have random noise that could not be systematically captured by Legendre regression with small number of polynomials.
So I looked at SDE discretizations and noticed that for each level of volatility coefficient and volatility exponent discretized at a particular dt (time step), there is a certain threshold level of SDE variable below which diffusion term exceeds the value of SDE variable and higher order derivatives exceed the successive lower derivatives and the whole thing has a chance of becoming unstable when normal random number drawn for simulation is high. Ideally below this level SDE time step for simulation has to be decreased so that diffusion term(or expected value of diffusion term) remains smaller than the SDE original variable there. I did not want to selectively decrease the step size since I was working with arrays in matlab and dealing separately with each simulation path would greatly slow down the speed. So below the calculated threshold, I just turned diffusion to zero and only allowed exact drift to appear in the SDE simulation. This would very slightly alter the distribution close to zero but remove a large amount of randomness in SDE parameters due to instabilities when diffusion term all of a sudden jumps very close to zero and becomes too large in simulation.

For example our mean reverting SDE is given as

\[dX_t\,=\, \kappa \,(\theta\,-\,X_t) \, dt \, + \, \sigma \, {X_t}^{\gamma} \, dZ_t \]  

ideally our discretization should be fine enough that differential term containing noise is smaller than the SDE variable \(X_t\) itself. For that to happen, we determine the threshold where both are expected to be equal as (I am using first order formula and mean reversion based formulas or variance based formulas could possibly lead to slightly better thresholds)(I noticed that higher derivatives also increase successive smaller derivatives very close to the same threshold point meaning this is the point where SDE can become unstable)

\[ \, \bar{X} \, =\,  \, \sigma \, {\bar{X_t}}^{\gamma} \, \sqrt{\Delta t} E[|Z|] \]  
Where \(|Z|\) is one sided Z that is either positive or negative.  where \(E[|Z|]\,=\, \sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}\)
so 
\[ \,\bar{X}\,=\, \left[ \, \sigma \, \sqrt{\Delta t} \sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}} \right]^{\frac{1}{1\,-\,\gamma}} \]

for example for \(\Delta t \, =\, \frac{1}{64} \) and \(\sigma\,=\,.75\)

for \(\gamma\,=\,.5\),   \(\bar{X}\,=\, 0.005595\)
for \(\gamma\,=\,.6\),   \(\bar{X}\,=\, 0.001530\)
for \(\gamma\,=\,.7\),   \(\bar{X}\,=\, 1.7634e-04\)
for \(\gamma\,=\,.8\),   \(\bar{X}\,=\, 2.341e-06\)
for \(\gamma\,=\,.9\),   \(\bar{X}\,=\,5.4842e-12\)

so if for particular \(\gamma\) and \(\sigma\) and \(\Delta t\), the value of  \(\bar{X}\) is not acceptably small enough, the discretization has to be refined selectively for paths that are smaller than this threshold to make sure that SDE properly captures the density of SDE. Obviously for paths in SDe simulation where SDE variable is larger than this threshold, stability is not an issue and we can freely use high order discretization schemes.
I did not refine my SDE but below the specific threshold, I just did not include the possibly unstable diffusion term and only used the analytic drift. This would extremely slightly alter the density close to zero but make the density parameters a lot more stable. 
With these changes, I was able to get the following R-squared with ten Legendre polynomials on a sample of 500 densities. I am using eight parameters for the hermite series of densities and one last parameter for squared root of volatility (that is equal to 0.995871326780030)

Brief Introduction to Legendre Polynomial Regression


Legendre polynomials are orthogonal polynomials associated with uniform density with support between -1 and +1. This means that integral of product of one Legendre polynomial with any other Legendre polynomial with uniform density weight in the integral over the support of uniform density between -1 and +1 would go to zero. 

We present mth Legendre Polynomial as \(L_m(.)\). For our Legendre regressions, we are only working with Legendre polynomials of Uniform density supported between -1 and +1, so we will universally use the notation   \(L_m(U)\). 

Legendre polynomial regression works very well not only for random variables over finite support, it can work equally well for finding deterministic relationships  between different variables that arise in physical processes and chemistry or physics experiments. 

Orthogonal polynomials work great for finding relationships between different random or deterministic variables since we standardize all variables involved in regression by mapping them over orthogonal polynomials of the same appropriate densities. Correlations can exist only among orthogonal polynomials of the same order. It is like decomposing various variables into the same common set of different standardized units/currencies (with different coefficient weights for all different variables)  where correlation can exist only among the same standardized units/currencies and then finding relationships between common standardized units/currencies across various variables. 

First few Legendre polynomials are given as

\[L_0(U)\,=\,1\]
\[L_1(U)\,=\,U\]
\[L_2(U)\,=\,1/2 \,\,(3 \,U^2\,-\,1) \]
\[L_3(U)\,=\,1/2 \,\,(5 \,U^3\,-\,3 \, U) \]
\[L_4(U)\,=\,1/8 \,\,(35 \,U^4\,-\,3 \, U^2 \,+\,3\,) \]


Finding Legendre Polynomial Series of a Continuous Variable

In this section we study how to represent data of various continuous variables (over their respective finite domains) as a respective series in orthogonal Legendre polynomials of Uniform density. For example a data variables X can be converted into a Legendre polynomial series over uniform as

\[\, X(U)\,=\, al_0 \, + \, al_1 \, L_1(U) \,+ \, al_2 \, L_2(U) \,+ \ldots \,+ \, al_N \, L_N(U) \, \] 

This can be done by choosing coefficients of the Legendre series so that moments of associated Legendre Series match with the moments of the associated variables. 

This can also be easily done with regression of data variables over discretized version of Uniform density to find the coefficients of Legendre series of a data variable. Please note this regression to convert a continuous variable (with support on finite domains) into a series in Legendre polynomials of uniform density is an intermediate step and is different from our final goal of a different regression to find relationship of a variable with various other variables through Legendre regression.  

We suppose there are a total of P data observations of random variable X whose Legendre-Series we want to find. In order to find coefficients of Legendre-Series, we have to regress each of the P data observations on N Legendre polynomials of a discretized uniform density points associated with each of the data observation therefore grid size of discretized uniform density should also be P(equal to P data observations). We also know that probability mass of each data observation is \(\frac{1}{P}\), so the probability mass of each grid cell on standard uniform discretized grid should also be \(\frac{1}{P}\). We make this discretized uniform density over P points by dividing the support of uniform density between -1 and 1 into P equal grid cells and discretize uniform density into P points that are centers of these grid cells. (Ideally we would also do a moment matching and slightly alter this discretized density to make sure its moments match with moments of the uniform density).  This way we can make a symmetric grid around median of uniform density that can be mapped on data observations through equivalent CDF.

In order to do the regression of P data observations of a continuous variable on P points of the discretized uniform density, we have to first associate each data observation with an appropriate point on the discretized uniform density. In order to do this, we sort the data observations in an ascending order and then associate nth member of this sorted array in ascending order with nth data point on discretized uniform density already in an ascending order. Once we have made this association of sorted data observations with discretized uniform density, we can re-order the data observations in original order (before sorting) or any other order carrying the associated discretized uniform density point along the same re-ordering that is applied to data. 

Once we have associated each data point with discretized uniform density by sorting the data, we simply regress data points on Legendre polynomial values calculated at particular discretized uniform density grid points in a least square fashion to find regression coefficients.  These regression coefficents represent the coefficients of Legendre polynomials in the Legendre-series.

If there are P data points, and we use N Legendre polynomials to represent the Legendre-Series of X, then our regression equation for each pth data point would be

\begin{equation}
X_p\,=\, al_0 \, + \,al_1\, L_1(U_p)\,+ \,al_2\, L_2(U_p)\,+ \ldots \,+\,al_N\, L_N(U_p)\,
\end{equation}

with the matrix equation given as

\begin{equation}
\begin{bmatrix} 1 & L_1(U_1) & L_2(U_1) & ..... & L_N(U_1) \\ 
1 & L_1(U_2) & L_2(U_2) & ..... & L_N(U_2) \\ ... & ... & ... & ... \\1 & L_1(U_p) & L_2(U_p) & ..... & L_N(U_p) \\  ... & ... & ... & ... & ... \\ 
1 & L_1(U_P) & L_2(U_P) & ... & L_N(U_P) 
\end{bmatrix}   \begin{bmatrix} al_0 \\ al_{1} \\ al_{2} \\ ... \\ al_{N} \end{bmatrix} \\ \,=\, \begin{bmatrix} X_1 \\ X_2 \\ ...  \\  X_p \\ ... \\  X_P \end{bmatrix}
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
\boldsymbol{L \, a_l}\,=\,\boldsymbol{x}
\end{equation}

and we solve this equation with least squares as

\begin{equation}
\boldsymbol{a_l}\,=\,\boldsymbol{{\left(L^T\, L \right)}^{-1}\,L^T\, x }
\end{equation}


Preparing Data For Legendre Polynomial Regression of a Continuous Variable on Several Other Continuous Variables

In order to Legendre-regress a continuous variable on several other variables, we have to first map observations of all the variables in regression sample on their respective discretized uniform densities. In Legendre regression, we do not need the coefficients of Legendre series of any variable in the regression and we only need the values of Legendre polynomials calculated at the values of discretized uniform density points associated with the observations. 

Since we do not need coefficients of Legendre series of any regressor in the Legendre polynomial regression, we do not need to do the regression described in previous section even though it is a good idea to do the regression described in previous section and find out the associated R-squared to see that the regressor can be well captured by the Legendre series of the variable and also find out if Legendre-regression is appropriate for a particular set of variables.

All we need to do to prepare data for Legendre regression of one variable on several other regressors is to first construct discretized version of the Uniform density with P grid points for each of the regressor variables. Sort observations of each data variable in an ascending order array and associate them to the same element of array of discretized uniform density. You can then change the order of observations of data variables back to original order but they would carry the association between sorted arrays with them. You can see that it is a mapping between two densities with equal members where data members of one density in ascending order are mapped on data members of another density in the same ascending order.   

I will give a very small concrete example with hypothetical data of only a few points for regression over three variables X, Y and W. Suppose there are only five data observations of each variable(We will usually need large data samples but this small hypothetical data is to simply explain the algorithm of mapping on uniform density)

For five data members, the discretized uniform density without any moment matching would be  \([-.8,  -.4,  0,  .4,  .8]\)

Suppose our five joint observations of X, Y and W are given as

The above example shows how to associate a regression variable with appropriate appropriate discretized uniform density. This method without actually finding the Legendre series coefficients is helpful when we have the whole sample of data observations however we would need to know the Legendre series of a variable after regression when we would have single observations of random variables in after-regression predictions. So we could map a whole sample of observations on discretized uniform density that would be used for finding parameters of a regression but in later calculations when single observations of variables would be given to predict the resulting variable, we would usually need the knowledge of coefficients of Legendre series of each parameter.


Doing Legendre Polynomial Regression

Suppose we want to Legendre-regress a variable V on other variables X, Y and their product XY. Let us suppose that we have a total of P joint data observations of above three regressors  and we include N Legendre polynomials for each of the three explanatory variable in the regression. And we also have P joint observations of the target regressand. Then, for each of the pth \((p\,=\,1, \ldots,P\,)\) data observation for each variable we would need to find the corresponding point on their respective uniform densities as we outlined in previous section. The corresponding values of uniform variable for pth observation of X, Y and XY would be represented as  \(U_{X_{p}}\) and \(U_{Y_{p}}\) and \(U_{XY_{p}}\). Then we would find first N Legendre polynomial values for each of the inverted        \(U_{X_{p}}\) and \(U_{Y_{p}}\) and and \(U_{XY_{p}}\).

It is also possible that sometimes, we are given a Legendre series for each of the variables X, Y and their XY and we might have to invert it for every pth observation  of X, Y and XY to find  \(U_{X_{p}}\) and \(U_{Y_{p}}\) and \(U_{XY_{p}}\) from the following equations 

\[\, X(U_X)\,=\, al_0 \, + \, al_1 \, L_1(U_X) \,+ \, al_2 \, L_2(U_X) \,+ \ldots \,+ \, al_N \, L_N(U_X) \, \] 
\[\, Y(U_Y)\,=\, bl_0 \, + \, bl_1 \, L_1(U_X) \,+ \, bl_2 \, L_2(U_Y) \,+ \ldots \,+ \, bl_N \, L_N(U_Y) \, \] 
\[\, XY(U_{XY})\,=\, cl_0 \, + \, cl_1 \, L_1(U_{XY}) \,+ \, cl_2 \, L_2(U_{XY}) \,+ \ldots \,+ \, cl_N \, L_N(U_{XY}) \, \] 


The final regression equation would be given in the matrix form as

\begin{equation}
\begin{bmatrix} 1 & L_1(U_{X_1}) & \ldots & L_N(U_{X_1}) & L_1(U_{Y_1}).& \ldots.. & L_N(U_{Y_1}) & L_1(U_{XY_1}).& \ldots.. & L_N(U_{XY_1}) \\ 
1 & L_1(U_{X_2}) & \ldots & L_N(U_{X_2}) & L_1(U_{Y_2}).& \ldots.. & L_N(U_{Y_2}) & L_1(U_{XY_2}).& \ldots.. & L_N(U_{XY_2})  \\ \ldots & \ldots & \ldots & \ & \ldots & \ldots.. & \ldots \\   1 & L_1(U_{X_p}) & \ldots & L_N(U_{X_p}) & L_1(U_{Y_p}) & \ldots & L_N(U_{Y_p}) & L_1(U_{XY_p}).& \ldots.. & L_N(U_{XY_p}) \\ \ldots & \ldots & \ldots & \ & \ldots & \ldots.. & \ldots \\
1 & L_1(U_{X_P}) & \ldots & L_N(U_{X_P})  & L_1(U_{Y_P})  & \ldots &  L_N(U_{Y_P}) & L_1(U_{XY_P}).& \ldots.. & L_N(U_{XY_P}) \\
\end{bmatrix}   \begin{bmatrix} \beta_0 \\ \beta_{X,1} \\ \ldots \\ \beta_{X,N} \\ \beta_{Y,1}\\\dots \\ \beta_{Y,N} \\ \beta_{XY,1}\\\dots \\ \beta_{XY,N} \end{bmatrix} \\ \,=\, \begin{bmatrix} V_1 \\ V_2 \\ ...  \\  V_p \\ ... \\  V_P \end{bmatrix}
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
\boldsymbol{L \, \beta}\,=\,\boldsymbol{v}
\end{equation}

and we solve this equation with least squares to find the regression coefficients of equation as

\begin{equation}
\boldsymbol{\beta}\,=\,\boldsymbol{{\left(L^T\, L \right)}^{-1}\,L^T\, v }
\end{equation}

since there are three random variable regressors and Legendre-Series of each random variable includes Legendre polynomials terms up to Nth order and there are a total of P joint data observations, then matrix \(\boldsymbol{L} \) has matrix size as  \( \text{P  X (1 + 3 N)} \) , \(\boldsymbol{\beta}\) has array size of \(\text{(1 +  3 N ) X 1} \) and \(\boldsymbol{v}\) has array size of \(\,\text{P X 1} \).

You can see example of Legendre regression in many of the matlab programs I have recently posted on the forum.

The first routine is  [Lu] = LegendrePoly(Order)  that takes in an argument order of Legendre polynomial as input. If for example this input argument order is equal to ten, output Lu will return a matrix containing coefficients of zeroth to tenth Legendre polynomials.  

The second helpful routine is [Wl] = EvalLegendreArrayL0(U,Order) . It takes two arguments as input. First argument U is an array of Uniform values (that might be associated with values of discretized uniform density underlying a variable). Second argument is Order of Legendre arrays. If for example input argument Order is ten, this function returns a matrix containing zeroth to tenth Legendre polynomial evaluated at the array of Uniforms. If input array of U (uniforms)has a size of 100 and Order is 10, it will return a 100 X 11 array.

function [Lu] = LegendrePoly(Order)

Lu(1:Order+1,1:Order+1)=0.0;
Lu(1,1)=1.0;
Lu(2,2)=1.0;
for n=2:Order
    
    for m=1:n
       Lu(n+1,m+1)=Lu(n+1,m+1)+Lu(n,m)*(2*(n-1)+1)/((n-1)+1);
    end
    for m=1:n-1
       Lu(n+1,m)=Lu(n+1,m)-Lu(n-1,m)*(n-1)/((n-1)+1);
    end
end
Lu;
end

Introduction to Math AI With a Case Study Towards Instantaneous Calculation of Densities of SDEs From Their Parameters

Context

We have been working with Hermite series/Z-series representation of densities for at least three years now. Using Hermite-Series/Z-series, we can represent a very large class of densities extremely faithfully with just 6-8 coefficients of the respective series. Most of the densities we come across in finance including Generalized Gamma density, most non-central chi-squared type densities, lognormal densities and large class of densities that are similar but cannot be described by three parameter classic distributions, can easily be captured using just 6-8 coefficients of respective Z-series/Hermite Series. There might be some exceptions when higher moments of some of these densities might be too large and then we might need to represent them with ten coefficients of the Z-series/Hermite-Series for example when variance of lognormal densities is too high. At any time along their evolution, densities of most SDEs that we usually encounter in finance can easily be captured by a Hermite-series/Z-series described only by six to eight coefficients.

In parallel with our work on finding hermite series of densities, we have been able to develop orthogonal polynomial regression methods which are extremely successful in capturing relationships between various random or deterministic variables.

Extracting useful information from many interacting random variables is a subject of very intensive research and many researchers like to use deep neural networks extensively for that purpose. We aim to combine our research on orthogonal polynomial regressions and parsimonious analytic representation of densities of random variables with a few coefficients and densities based Bayesian methods to efficiently solve most of these problems in probability theory and statistics of continuous random variables that arise in practice. We expect that our Math AI methods would be more precise than deep network methods and would require far almost trivial computational effort in training algorithms since calculating the parameters of orthogonal polynomials only requires least square regressions and is almost computationally trivial.

As a demonstration case study we decided to solve a very difficult problem of finding densities of SDEs from their parameters just as instantaneously as is done by neural network methods. This is a very good test case study since densities of SDEs can be calculated very precisely and we can see how good our Math AI methods are to capture these densities. At the same time this example is very relevant since in a lot of practical applications where we deal with interaction of many random variables since we can reproduce the densities of those random variables very quickly from realized values and it will be based on a previous learning process applied to data using orthogonal polynomials and we would quickly find values of variables of interest also based on previous orthogonal polynomial regressions. We can do more precise calculations of variables of interest with several orders of smaller computational effort as compared to what is used in network parameters learning process of deep networks. This can be used in for example in a large number of internet applications that make use of consumer data to make quick decisions that tailor all sort of products, offerings, advertisements and many other things for the specific customer. Particularly in finance, Math AI could help us make all sort of risk and investment decisions very quickly in all different sort of market scenarios. 

Application of Math AI to Calculation of Densities of SDEs

We aim to find density of SDEs of a particular class from the parameters of the SDE as input to the algorithm. We choose the following SDE 

\begin{align}
& dY_t\,=\, \kappa \, (\theta \, -\, Y_t\,)\, dt\,+ \sigma \, {Y_t}^{\gamma} \, dZ(t) \\
& Y_0\,=\,y_0
\end{align}

The density of this SDE depends upon the parameters given as  \((y_0, \kappa, \theta, \sigma, \gamma, T) \) . For a particular combination of these parameters, we can easily simulate this density using monte carlo and represent it in terms of a eight coefficients Hermite series using regression of monte carlo sample on Hermite polynomials of discretized normal density. These coefficients would be different for all of the combinations of parameters of the SDE. Since out eight parsimonious coefficients of the particular density are derived from an analytic process, they would change smoothly with a change in parameters of the SDE. So we can use another Legendre orthogonal polynomial regression on SDE parameters to find these coefficients from the parameters of the SDE. So given SDE parameters, we would be able to quickly generate the coefficients of the particular SDE using Legendre coefficients and these coefficients could be used with Hermite polynomials to find the density of the SDE with particular parameters. 

Generation of Sample of SDE densities By Randomization over Input Parameters of the SDE

In order to represent coefficients of Hermite-series of densities of SDEs with Legendre polynomial series, we have to generate large number of Hermite-series coefficients samples over the given range of parameters of the SDEs. There are six parameters of the SDEs on which we want to regress Hermite-series coefficients. It is not possible to make a six dimensional uniform grid for these parameters. So we randomize and draw five dimensional uniforms( and treat time by mapping time grid on a uniform) and map them on range of SDE parameters. We also use antithetic sampling in drawing five dimensional uniforms. We make several thousand draws of five dimensional uniform parameters and simulate the SDE in time for each value of five dimensional input parameters. In the next section, we describe how to find Hermite series from monte carlo simulations of SDEs. 

Estimation of Hermite-series density From SDE Numerical Data

Estimation of coefficients of Hermite-Series From numerical data works by regressing data observations on hermite polynomials associated with a discrete grid version of standard normal density.  We know that in Hermite series of a random variable, our data observations of non-Gaussian random variable and the underlying standard normal random variable are mapped on each other through common CDF values.

Therefore, in this method first we have to construct a discrete grid on standard normal density so that number of discrete grid points on standard normal density match the total number of data observations and  probability mass associated with each grid cell matches the probability of each data observation and CDF of each discrete standard normal grid center matches the CDF of Hermite representation of each data point.

Construction of Discrete Normal Density on Data Grid And Regression of Non-Gaussian Random Variable Data Observations on Hermite Polynomials.

We suppose there are a total of P data observations of random variable X whose Hermite-Series we want to find. In order to find coefficients of Hermite-Series, we have to regress each of the P data observation on N hermite polynomials of a discrete normal density point associated with the data observation therefore grid size of discrete normal density should also be P. We also know that probability mass of each data observation is \(\frac{1}{P}\), so the probability mass of each grid cell on standard normal discrete grid should also be \(\frac{1}{P}\). The centres of these grid cells represent the discrete grid normal density points. In order to numerically construct this discrete standard normal grid of size P, we start out symmetrically from median and make boundaries of grid cells so that probability mass within each grid cell is \(\frac{1}{P}\). This way we can make a symmetric grid around median that can be mapped on data observations through equivalent CDF.

We regress our observations of non-Gaussian random variables on the Hermite polynomials calculated at these discrete grid normal density points. 
We have assumed that first derivative of non-Gaussian random variable with respect to standard normal random variable is positive, and it means that data can be re-ordered in increasing function (sorted in ascending order) and assigned to increasing order of grid points on discrete normal density grid points we constructed earlier.  

Once we have constructed discrete normal grid and associated each point on it with sorted data points, we simply regress data points on Hermite polynomial values calculated at particular standard normal discrete grid point on the associated sorted data in a least square fashion to find regression coefficients that also represent the coefficients of Hermite polynomials in the Hermite-series.

If there are P data points, and we use N Hermite polynomials to represent the Hermite-Series of X, then our regression equation for each pth data point would be

\begin{equation}
X_p\,=\, ah_0 \, + \,ah_1\, H_1(Z_p)\,+ \,ah_2\, H_2(Z_p)\,+ \ldots \,+\,ah_N\, H_N(Z_p)\,
\end{equation}

with the matrix equation given as

Standardization Of Hermite-Series Coefficients

We want to regress each of the \(ah_n\) on Legendre polynomials of SDE input parameters \((y_0, \kappa, \theta, \sigma, \gamma, T) \) and all their cross products with first powers. We standardize each of the SDE coefficients in sample with square root of variance of the SDE. This variance is given as

\begin{equation}
Var_{Y}\,=\, E \left[ {Y}^2\right]\,=\, \sum\limits_{n=1}^{N} \,{ah_n}^2 n! 
\end{equation} 

and we standardize all coefficients with this variance as

\begin{equation}
\bar{ah_n}\,=\, \frac{ah_n}{\sqrt{ Var_{Y}}}
\end{equation} 

Please note that variance of density with these standardized coefficients would be unity. But we would also need to regress the square root of volatility on parameters that could be used to inverse this standardization back to original values of coefficients of Hermite-Series.
 
We do Legendre polynomial regression of eight coefficients and Square-root of volatility on Legendre polynomials of uniforms of input parameters associated with each observation of Hermite-Series Coefficients of the SDEs in the sample.

Doing Legendre Polynomial Regression

Re-Standardization of Hermite Coefficients

We find the Hermite Coefficients of an SDE from their parameters using the Legendre regression as given in previous section. We know that variance of density from these coefficients should be unity as they had been standardized prior to regression. However this variance would be very slightly different from one so we adjust the coefficients to retrieve unit volatility from the relationship

\begin{equation}
\bar{ah_n}\,=\, \frac{ah_n}{\, \sqrt{\sum\limits_{n=1}^{N} \,{ah_n}^2 n!}}
\end{equation} 

The regression of Hermite-Series parameters after Standardization and re-standardization after calculation of Hermite-series Coefficients as described above, further improves the match of resulting SDE density with True density.

In order to find the true values of these coefficients of Hermite-series of an SDE, we would have to multiply above standardized values of coefficients with square root of variance of the SDE. We also regress this square root of variance of SDE as a 9th regression along with eight regressions for each of the hermite-series coefficients. If this square root of variance of an SDE is known in closed form from other analytics, we can use that and multiply it to find the true coefficients of Hermite-series of the densities of SDEs.
