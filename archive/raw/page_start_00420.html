In cases of some ODEs that have complicated expressions in independent variable, many times addition of each next iterated integrals solution has increasing number of terms. For example, for a 2nd order ODE, in some cases solution of first double integral may have two terms, the solution to second third order integral would have three terms after integration and next fourth order integral may have four terms and so on. While each set of terms is well behaved and bounded collectively but when we collect lower powers across all integrals they seem to diverge in that specific power but the whole solution would still converge because higher powers cancel the diverging effect of lower powers but I am sure more intelligent mathematicians would want to work on how to analyze these expanding series(as solution of each higher integral has more terms that solution of previous lower integral. May be there could be an analogy with two dimensional series that each term in solution is itself a collection of many terms in a way that each increasing term has more terms in it than the previous term.) I hope that programs I posted would be helpful for intelligent mathematicians to analyze how to systematically simplify the growing term by term solution in a very simple form. If you do intelligent research, Please do quote my paper and reference to my programs and this Wilmott thread(Our own forum.) 

On the related question of solution of PDEs, there are several analytic techniques like many versions of separation of variables that transform the PDEs into ODEs that can easily be solved by the method of iterated integrals. here is initial reference https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Separation_of_variables and many people have done very advanced research on solution of navier stokes, fokker plank, Laplace equation, wave equation and many other PDEs after converting them into ODEs. I hope many other smart friends would want to explore doing more research in this area.
When trying to solve partial differential equations, if we can convert them into a set of ODEs, we can again solve the ODEs with method of iterated integrals to get a series solution in terms of arbitrary initial values. Many smart mathematicians would like to identify the closed from solution from the series solution. If that could be possible, it could result in a closed form solution to set of ODEs underlying the complete solution of the partial differential equation after choosing appropriate constants that would satisfy the boundary conditions in the closed form solution of the ODEs. This may always not be possible but I am sure it would be good to take advantage of this possibility whenever we can.

cuch, what method do you suggest for your problem [$]y'=\frac{2y}{t}[$] with [$]y(0)=y_0\ne 0[$] ?

(edit: cuch's initial condition added)


write as  [$]t'=\frac{t}{2y}[$] with [$]t(y_0)=0[$]  where [$]y_0\ne 0[$]
Personally, I tend to look at the problem:
A. Qualitative (analyse the ODE, determiinistic it has a solution (or not) and where).
B. Quantitative (get the solution: exact, numerical whatever).
In the above case (there are 3 other scenarios depending on t_0 and y_0) we can almost see it has no solution just be letting t ->0. (the left and right sides give different answers. In fact, writing [$] ty' = 2y, y(0) = y_0 ( y_0 \ne 0)[$] it look more like a classical singular perturbation problem.
So, to answer the question, I used Picard and it diverges after one iteration (it is clear why because it uses integrals and series). We can pinpoint the reason that it is not converging and that gives insights. NB the iterates do not involve y.
I have applied iterated integral as well and it too explodes. However, It involves y itself which is begging the question and leads to circular reasoning.. Likewise, perturbation is based on Taylor expansions which are always scary. Numerically, they are unstable (that's why RK methods were born). I challenge the feasability of asymptotic expansions (the are popular in physics).
So, it seems proofs and counter-proofs are possible with Picard.

What's the idea behind [$]t' = t/2y[$]?

Thus any example in which solution depends on all values of f on [ 0 , T ] is a counterexample to amin's idea.
