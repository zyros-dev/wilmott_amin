Friends, I had promised to explain the details of the method used to write the matlab program for finding conditional density of a continuous random variable conditional upon the values taken by several other correlated continuous random variables. In the following few posts, I will write about preliminaries and context of the method and then move on to details of conditional density calculation algorithm.

I am presenting it as a competing framework for conditional density regressions and other related variants of the type that appear in quantitative finance, natural sciences, and medicine. But all of this has to be considered in the broader framework of orthogonal polynomials. For example, I have worked with hermite orthogonal polynomials only in order to deal  with tailed densities that can be represented by hermite polynomials. This is a class of densities of continuous random variables of the type chi-squared, noncentral chi-squared, generalized chi-squared, gamma , generalized gamma and many others. I am just quoting above densities as an example and our method is a super-set and works with many similar tailed densities that cannot be represented by any of the above closed form density classes but still can be represented by a hermite polynomial series. We do not use any parameters or closed form distributions of any of the above analytic density classes in our conditional density calculations and analytics are only done using underlying Hermite orthogonal polynomial series.

However for many other specialized problems with random variables of different density types, you would have to use some other appropriate orthogonal polynomial type. For example densities on a finite domain (usually without tails) of beta types and many other similar to beta density can be represented by Legendre orthogonal polynomials of uniform random variable. And we would have to calculate correlations between Legendre orthogonal polynomials of uniform density underlying Legendre-Series of different random variables. Similarly for many other positive densities we could use Laguerre orthogonal polynomials of gamma density as underlying basis to represent the random variable and find correlations between Laguerre orthogonal polynomials of gamma type underlying Laguerre-series of various random variables. Similarly sinusoidal functions could be represented by other appropriate orthogonal polynomials of their respective density and could be used to find correlations between those orthogonal polynomials to find the conditional density of various variables. Again my background is in mathematical finance and therefore I have worked with hermite polynomials based densities since this is the type of random variables that mostly arise in finance but you would have to choose appropriate orthogonal polynomials to represent the random variables in the data under question in your research.

Even though I will soon give preliminaries about the method but briefly our method consists in representing a random variable in terms of a series in appropriate class of orthogonal polynomials. Then we find correlations between these orthogonal polynomials just like Pearson correlation is calculated. Correlations exist between orthogonal polynomials of the same order between different random variables and there are no correlations between orthogonal polynomials of different order across different random variables due to orthogonality of polynomials. But as opposed to one set of Gaussian correlations that we usually deal with, there can be, for example, seven sets of correlations if the hermite-series consists of first seven underlying orthogonal hermite polynomials representing various random variables. For the relevant class of variables that can be represented by hermite polynomials, our method gives very good estimates of conditional expected value that is very close to true conditional value and we have recently augmented it with conditional density generation algorithm that gives very good estimates of conditional density of the random variables conditional on values taken by several other correlated random variables. We expect that there are many other unique but related problems that can be solved by the method after making some minor modifications that are necessary to deal with peculiarities of the unique problems.

Since it is a polynomial based method, it would usually be assumed that it would suffer from curse of dimensionality and it would be difficult to find densities of target random variables conditional on more than a handful of 5-6 correlated variables but we have devised a simple algorithm that repeatedly converts N dimensional problem into a two dimensional problem to calculate appropriate conditionality parameters and our computational complexity of finding conditional density given N correlated random variables is that of N two dimensional problems which is extremely scalable to very high dimensions.

This is a very fast algorithm for conditional density calculation and in my matlab example programs, it takes most of the time to generate underlying simulated SDE data and training time is only to find out Pearson type correlations between hermite polynomials of same order between different random variables and some other minor calculations. And it gives conditional density results very close to the true density simulated again by independent monte carlo simulation. Some minor mismatch is mostly, but not always, attributed to monte carlo randomness since in my experiments I tried to use not too large number of monte carlo simulation paths.

I am just presenting the method as a way to find true conditional values and conditional density (and other related variants based on conditionality) in data science problems that arise in natural sciences, finance and medicine and it may not be alternative to all types of neural networks. However, I would suspect that improvised versions of the method could be used in fast training of many other types of neural networks but that is beyond the scope of current research.

Introduction to Z-Series and Hermite-Series

An extremely broad class of continuous non-Gaussian random variables can be represented as a power series in base standard random variables with very simple densities. Standard Gaussian and Standard Uniform are examples of two simple random variables whose power series could be used to find simple and convenient analytical representation of non-Gaussian random variables within appropriate classes. Most of the continuous non-Gaussian random variables of Log Normal distribution, generalized gamma distribution, and non-central chi-squared distribution can be represented as a power series in standard Gaussian random variable. Similarly continuous random variables belonging to beta distribution and many other distributions defined on a finite interval can be represented as a power series in standard Uniform random variable.

Representation of Non-Gaussian Random Variables in terms of a Taylor Series in Standard Gaussian called Z-Series

We can represent a large class of non-Gaussian random variables in terms of a Taylor Series in Standard Gaussian Random variable around its median \(Z_0\,=\,0\).

\begin{equation}\label{2.1.1}
X(Z)\,=\,X(Z_0) \,+\, \sum\limits_{n=1}^{N}\, \frac{d^nX}{dZ^n}\, \frac{{Z}^n}{n!} 
\end{equation}

 We want to mention that above relationship between X and Z holds through equivalent Cumulative Distribution Function of both random variables and following two equations hold for any related \(X_m\) and \(Z_m\) in \(X_m(Z_m)\)

\begin{equation}\label{2.1.2}
Z_{m} \,=\, {\Phi}^{-1}(P_X(X_m)) 
\end{equation}

and 

\begin{equation}\label{2.1.3}
X_{m} \,=\, P^{-1}_X(\Phi(Z_m)) 
\end{equation}

where \(\Phi(.)\) and \(\Phi^{-1}(.)\) and \(P_X(.)\) and \({P}^{-1}_X(.)\) represent CDF and inverse CDF of standard Gaussian and non-Gaussian random variable X respectively. 


In most of the rest of this paper will represent the above series as

\begin{equation}\label{2.1.4}
X(Z)\,=\, \sum\limits_{n=0}^{N}\, a_n\, {Z}^n 
\end{equation}

where it is understood that 

\begin{equation}\label{2.1.5}
a_0\,=\, X(Z_0)\, 
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}\label{2.1.6}
a_n\,=\, \frac{1}{n!}\, \frac{d^nX}{dZ^n}\,,n\,\geq\,1 \, 
\end{equation}

In some of the applications of Z-series, we do not directly know the derivatives \(\frac{d^nX}{dZ^n}\, \) and we directly estimate the coefficients \(a_n\,\) from moments of the non-Gaussain random variables or through other numerical methods but equations (\ref{2.1.5} and \ref{2.1.6}) nevertheless hold. 

Hermite-Series Random Variables Representation

There is another representation of many non-Gaussian Continuous random variables in terms of Hermite Polynomials of standard Gaussian random variable.
\begin{equation}\label{2.2.1}
X(Z)\,=\, \sum\limits_{n=0}^{N}\, ah_n\, H_n(Z) 
\end{equation}

where \(H_n(Z)\) represents nth hermite polynomial as a function of standard normal random variable.
This Hermite series representation of a particular order is algebraically equivalent to Z-series representation of the same order discussed in section \ref{sec2.1}. There are many analytics on non-Gaussian random variables like transformation of random variables using Taylor series and multiplication of random variables where a Z-series representation is more useful while there are some other analytics associated with correlations, variances and conditional expectation of non-Gaussian random variables where Hermite-Series representation would be more useful. In many of our numerical algorithms, we continue to switch between Z-Series of a random variable and its equivalent Hermite series.


Statistical Properties of Z-Series and Hermite-Series Random variables


In order to find probability distribution function and cumulative distribution function of Z-series and Hermite Series random variables, we have to first invert the formulas (\ref{2.1.4}) and (\ref{2.2.1}) to find Z(X). We have discussed this inversion algorithm in section \ref{sec2.9} later in the paper. We want to emphasize that some statistical properties are easier to find in Z-Series form while Hermite-Series form is convenient for many other statistical properties.


probability distribution function of X is given through change of variable formula with standard normal density as

\begin{equation}\label{2.3.1}
p_X(X) \,=\, p_Z(Z(X))\,  |\frac{dZ}{dX}|
\end{equation}
cumulative distribution function of X is found through calculation of cumulative distribution function of associated value of Gaussian random variable as

\begin{equation}\label{2.3.2}
P_X(X) \,=\,\int\limits_{-\infty}^{Z(X)} p_Z(Z)\, dZ
\end{equation}

Mean of the Hermite-series distribution in given as

\begin{equation}\label{2.3.3}
\text{Mean}= E \left[X \right] \,=\, ah_0
\end{equation}

In terms of Z-series, mean is a series in even coefficients taken till final index N of the Z-series as

\begin{equation}\label{2.3.4}
\text{Mean}= E \left[X \right] \,=\,a_0\,+\, a_2 \, + \, 3\, a_4 \, + 15 \, a_6 + \ldots
\end{equation}

Median of the Z-series random variable is given as

\begin{equation}\label{2.3.5}
\text{Median}=a_0
\end{equation}
Variance of the Hermite-Series random variables is given as

\begin{equation}\label{2.3.6}
Var(X)= E \left[ \,(X\,-\mu(X))^2 \right] \,=\, E \left[ \left(\sum\limits_{n=0}^{N}\, ah_n\, H_n(Z)\,-ah_0 \, \right)^2 \, \right] \,=\, \sum\limits_{n=1}^{N}\, n! \, {ah_n}^2 
\end{equation}

Moments of the Z-series random variables are calculated as

\begin{equation}\label{2.3.7}
\mu_m(X)= E \left[ \,(X(Z))^m \right] \,=\,\, E \left[ \left(\sum\limits_{n=0}^{N}\, a_n\, Z^n \,\right)^m \right] \,
\end{equation}

In practice, the above moments are calculated through repeated series product of coefficients of a Z-series with itself and later substituting the expected values of powers of standard Gaussian in the expression.


Equivalence of Hermite-Series and Z-Series Random Variables

Nth order Z-series and Nth order Hermite-Series share a common span of Nth order polynomials so any random variable that can be represented with Nth order Z-series, can also be represented by a Hermite-Series up to Nth order Hermite Polynomial. In fact, once coefficients of Z-series are known, it is easily possible to find coefficients of equivalent Hermite-Series and Vice versa and this is just consequence of simple algebra.\par
If we have Z-series representation of a non-Gaussian random variable is given, we can easily find coefficients of its Hermite-Series and vice versa.

If we represent a Z-series as 
\begin{equation}\label{2.4.1}
X(Z)\,=\, \sum\limits_{n=0}^{N}\, a_n\, {Z}^n 
\end{equation}
and a hermite series as
\begin{equation}\label{2.4.2}
X(Z)\,=\, \sum\limits_{n=0}^{N}\, ah_n\, H_n(Z) 
\end{equation}
then for \(N=7\) (for \(N\, < \,7\), omit any coefficients that are larger than \(N\)), we can find Hermite-series coefficients from Z-Series coefficients as 


\begin{align}\label{2.4.3}
& ah_0\,=\,a_0\,+\,a_2\,+\,3\,a_4\,+\,15\,a_6\\
& ah_1\,=\,a_1\,+3\,a_3\,+\,15\,a_5\,+\,105\,a_7\\
& ah_2\,=\,a_2\,+\,6\,a_4\,+\,45\,a_6\\
& ah_3\,=\,a_3\,+\,10\,a_5\,+\,105\,a_7\\
& ah_4\,=\,a_4\,+\,15\,a_6\\
& ah_5\,=\,a_5\,+\,21\,a_7\\
& ah_6\,=\,a_6\\
& ah_7\,=\,a_7\\
\end{align}

similarly, we can find Z-series coefficients from Hermite-Series coefficients as 


\begin{align}\label{2.4.4}
& a0\,=\,ah0\,-\,ah_2\,+\,3\,ah_4\,-\,15\,ah_6\\
& a_1\,=\,ah_1\,-\,3\,ah_3\,+\,15\,ah_5\,-\,105\,ah_7\\
& a_2\,=\,ah_2\,-6\,ah_4\,+\,45\,ah_6\\
& a_3\,=\,ah_3\,-10\,ah_5\,+\,105\,ah_7\\
& a_4\,=\,ah_4\,-15 \, ah_6\\
& a_5\,=\,ah_5\,-21 \, ah_7\\
& a_6\,=\,ah_6\\
& a_7\,=\,ah_7\\
\end{align}


Algorithm for Inversion of Hermite-Series/Z-Series Random Variables

Many times in our analytics, we know the coefficients of Z-series associated with a random variable as

\begin{equation}\label{2.9.1}
X(Z)\,=\, \sum\limits_{n=0}^{N}\, a_n\, {Z}^n 
\end{equation}

and we need to find the value of Z associated with a particular observation of X. Suppose we have a particular observation of \(X\,=\,X_0\), then associated value of \(Z(X_0)\) would be the one that solves the following equation 
\begin{equation}\label{2.9.2}
X_0\,=\, \sum\limits_{n=0}^{N}\, a_n\, {Z}^n 
\end{equation}

In order to find the inverse Z of a particualr value \(X_0\), we divide the range of standard normal random variables between -7 to +7 into fourteen equal intervals. We call \(Z_{u}\) as upper boundary and \(Z_{d}\) as lower boundary of each interval. 

We loop over these intervals one by one and find the values 
\begin{align}\label{2.9.3}
& X(Z_d)\,=\, \sum\limits_{n=0}^{N}\, a_n\, {Z_d}^n \\
& X(Z_u)\,=\, \sum\limits_{n=0}^{N}\, a_n\, {Z_u}^n 
\end{align}

and choose the interval where

\begin{equation}\label{2.9.4}
X(Z_{d})<= X_0 <= X(Z_{u}) 
\end{equation}

Once we have bracketed the value of Z associated with \(X_0\) in an interval, we find 
\begin{equation}\label{2.9.5}
Z_{mid} \,= \, \frac{1}{2} (Z_{d}+Z_{u}) 
\end{equation}
and solve for 
\begin{align}\label{2.9.6}
& X_{mid}\,=\, \sum\limits_{n=0}^{N}\, a_n\, \left( Z_{mid} \right)^n \\ 
\end{align}

and if \(X_0 > X_{mid} \), we halve the bracketed interval by choosing 

 \(Z_{d}=Z_{mid}\) and  \(Z_{u}=Z_{u} \) 
 and if \(X_0 < X_{mid} \), we halve the bracketed interval by choosing \(Z_{u}=Z_{mid}\) and  \(Z_{d}=Z_{d} \) 

We continue to repeat the above steps until the chosen value of \(Z_{mid}\) is so that \(X(Z_{mid})\) is within error tolerance of \(X_0\) to be inverted.

We have posted a matlab program CalculateZgivenXAndHermiteSeriesBisection with its dependencies on Wilmott forum \footnote{\url{viewtopic.php?t=99702\&start=2220\#p880141}}{Post 2223} to find Z underlying a particular realization of hermite-series random variable when coefficients of a Hermite-series random variable are given.


Estimation of Z-series Coefficients From Numerical Data

Estimation of coefficients of Hermite-Series From numerical data works by regressing data observations on hermite polynomials associated with a discrete grid version of standard normal density.  We know that in Hermite series of a random variable, our data observations of non-Gaussian random variable and the underlying standard normal random variable are mapped on each other through common CDF values.

Therefore, in this method first we have to construct a discrete grid on standard normal density so that number of discrete grid points on standard normal density match the total number of data observations and  probability mass associated with each grid cell matches the probability of each data observation and CDF of each discrete standard normal grid center matches the CDF of Hermite representation of each data point.

Construction of Discrete Normal Density on Data Grid And Regression of Non-Gaussian Random Variable Data Observations on Hermite Polynomials.

We suppose there are a total of P data observations of random variable X whose Hermite-Series we want to find. In order to find coefficients of Hermite-Series, we have to regress each of the P data observation on N hermite polynomials of a discrete normal density point associated with the data observation therefore grid size of discrete normal density should also be P. We also know that probability mass of each data observation is \(\frac{1}{P}\), so the probability mass of each grid cell on standard normal discrete grid should also be \(\frac{1}{P}\). The centres of these grid cells represent the discrete grid normal density points. In order to numerically construct this discrete standard normal grid of size P, we start out symmetrically from median and make boundaries of grid cells so that probability mass within each grid cell is \(\frac{1}{P}\). This way we can make a symmetric grid around median that can be mapped on data observations through equivalent CDF.

We regress our observations of non-Gaussian random variables on the Hermite polynomials calculated at these discrete grid normal density points. 
We have assumed that first derivative of non-Gaussian random variable with respect to standard normal random variable is positive, and it means that data can be re-ordered in increasing function (sorted in ascending order) and assigned to increasing order of grid points on discrete normal density grid points we constructed earlier.  

Once we have constructed discrete normal grid and associated each point on it with sorted data points, we simply regress data points on Hermite polynomial values calculated at particular standard normal discrete grid point on the associated sorted data in a least square fashion to find regression coefficients that also represent the coefficients of Hermite polynomials in the Hermite-series.

If there are P data points, and we use N Hermite polynomials to represent the Hermite-Series of X, then our regression equation for each pth data point would be

\begin{equation}\label{3.2.1}
X_p\,=\, ah_0 \, + \,ah_1\, H_1(Z_p)\,+ \,ah_2\, H_2(Z_p)\,+ \ldots \,+\,ah_N\, H_N(Z_p)\,
\end{equation}

with the matrix equation given as

\begin{equation}\label{3.2.2}
\begin{bmatrix} 1 & H_1(Z_1) & H_2(Z_1) & ..... & H_N(Z_1) \\ 
1 & H_1(Z_2) & H_2(Z_2) & ..... & H_N(Z_2) \\ ... & ... & ... & ... \\1 & H_1(Z_p) & H_2(Z_p) & ..... & H_N(Z_p) \\  ... & ... & ... & ... & ... \\ 
1 & H_1(Z_P) & H_2(Z_P) & ... & H_N(Z_P) 
\end{bmatrix}   \begin{bmatrix} ah_0 \\ ah_{1} \\ ah_{2} \\ ... \\ ah_{N} \end{bmatrix} \\ \,=\, \begin{bmatrix} X_1 \\ X_2 \\ ...  \\  X_p \\ ... \\  X_P \end{bmatrix}
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}\label{3.2.3}
\boldsymbol{H \, a_h}\,=\,\boldsymbol{x}
\end{equation}

and we solve this equation with least squares as

\begin{equation}\label{3.2.4}
\boldsymbol{a_h}\,=\,\boldsymbol{{\left(H^T\, H \right)}^{-1}\,H^T\, x }
\end{equation}

Correlation, Covariance and Conditional Expectation Between Non-Gaussian Random variables through their Hermite-Series Representation


When we find Hermite series representation of a non-Gaussian random variable, we decompose it in terms of orthogonal Hermite polynomials of various orders. Due to this orthogonality property of different Hermite polynomials, any two non-Gaussian random variables with their Hermite-Series can only take correlations between Hermite polynomials of the same order across their respective Hermite-series and there are no correlations between Hermite polynomials of different order in the Hermite-Series of two random variables. So we no longer have a single correlation between two non-Gaussian random variables and we have to work with multiple correlations where each of the correlations is calculated between Hermite polynomials of the same order across Hermite-Series of two random variables. 

Similarly Variance, Covariance and conditional expectations are calculated separately between Hermite polynomials of each order in Hermite-Series of two random variables and then these variances, covariances and conditional expectations between each Hermite polynomial are added through specific relationships to find total variance, covariance and conditional expectations between two non-Gaussian random variables. 

Suppose we have two non-Gaussian correlated random variables X and Y whose uni-variate Hermite series are given respectively as
\begin{equation}\label{4.1}
X(Z_X)\,=\, \sum\limits_{n=0}^{N}\, bh_n\, H_n(Z_X)\, =\, \sum\limits_{n=0}^{N}\, X_n 
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}\label{4.2}
Y(Z_Y)\,=\, \sum\limits_{n=0}^{N}\, ch_n\, H_n(Z_Y) \, =\, \sum\limits_{n=0}^{N}\, Y_n 
\end{equation}

In rest of this section, we would deal with correlations, covariances and various cases of conditional expectations between above two non-Gaussian random variables X and Y.



Estimation of Correlations Between Hermite Polynomials of each Order in Hermite-Series of two non-Gaussian Random variables X and Y

We stress again that in Hermite-Series framework, there is no single correlation between two non-Gaussian random variables and separate correlations have to be calculated between Hermite polynomials of same order across Hermite-Series of two random variables. Once we have calculated these multiple correlations between Hermite polynomials of two random variables, We have to do further analytics with these separate correlations to find total covariances and total conditional expectations through relationships we dscribe in next few sections.

Suppose we have P joint observations of random variables \(X_p\) \(\,(p\,=\,1\, \ldots P)\) and \(Y_p\) \(\,(p\,=\,1\, \ldots P)\). From \(X_p\) and  \(Y_p\), We would have to invert equations below to find \(Z_{X,p}\) \(\,(p\,=\,1\, \ldots P)\) and \(Z_{Y,p}\) \(\,(p\,=\,1\, \ldots P)\)
 
\begin{equation}\label{4.1.1}
X_p\,=\, \sum\limits_{n=0}^{N}\, bh_n\, H_n(Z_{X,p})\,
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}\label{4.1.2}
Y_p\,=\, \sum\limits_{n=0}^{N}\, ch_n\, H_n(Z_{Y,p}) \,
\end{equation}
This has to be done by numerical inversion method we described in section (\ref{sec2.9}).
 
 we write the equation for the correlation between first Hermite polynomial of Hermite-Series random variables X and Y as
\begin{equation}\label{4.1.3}
\rho_{XY,1} \, = \,  \frac{\sum\limits_{p=1}^{P} \, Z_{X,p} \, \, Z_{Y,p} \,}{\sqrt{\sum\limits_{p=1}^{P} \, {Z_{X,p}}^2 \, \sum\limits_{p=1}^{P} \, \, {Z_{Y,p}}^2 \,}}
\end{equation}


Again, Please notice that for calculation of above equation, we first have to find the specific values of \(Z_{X,p}\) and  \(Z_{Y,p}\) that are associated with particular pth observation of  \(X_{p}\) and  \(Y_{p}\).

For Finding Correlation between second hermite polynomials, we write the equation for the correlation between second hermite polynomial of X and Y as

\begin{equation}\label{4.1.4}
\rho_{XY,2} \, = \,  \frac{\sum\limits_{p=1}^{P} \, ({Z_{X,p}}^2-1) \, \, ({Z_{Y,p}}^2-1) \,}{\sqrt{\sum\limits_{p=1}^{P} \, {(Z_{X,p}^2-1)}^2 \,\sum\limits_{p=1}^{P} \, \, {({Z_{Y,p}}^2-1})^2 \,}}
\end{equation}


similarly for nth Hermite polynomial correlations, we would write as

\begin{equation}\label{4.1.5}
\rho_{XY,n} \, = \,  \frac{\sum\limits_{p=1}^{P}\, H_n(Z_{X,p}) \, \, H_n(Z_{Y,p}) \,}{\sqrt{\sum\limits_{p=1}^{P} \, {H_n(Z_{X,p})}^2 \,\sum\limits_{p=1}^{P} \, \, {H_n(Z_{Y,p})}^2 \,}}
\end{equation}

Please note that correlations are related to joint expectations of values of only Hermite polynomials of same order across Hermite-series of two random variables. The coefficients of Hermite Polynomials in respective Hermite-Series do not play any role in calculation of correlations and are only required for determination of covariances between Hermite polynomials of two random variables.


Variances and Covariances Between Two Correlated Non-Gaussian Random variables X and Y

We learnt in previous sections that there are separate correlations between Hermite polynomials of each order in Hermite-Series of two non-Gaussian random variables due to orthogonality of Hermite polynomials. Therefore covariances also have to be calculated separately for Hermite polynomials of each order in Hermite Series of random variables. However due to orthogonality of Hermite polynomials, We can linearly add these separate Hermite covariances to find total covariance between two non-Gaussian random variables as we show in this section.   

We know univariate Hermite series of random variable Y and random variable X given in equations(\ref{4.1} and \ref{4.2}) . In next two equations below, we define \(X_n\) and \(Y_n\) as parts of X and Y that are associated with nth Herrmite polynomials.

\begin{equation}\label{4.2.1}
X(Z_X)\,=\, \sum\limits_{n=0}^{N}\, bh_n\, H_n(Z_X)\, =\, \sum\limits_{n=0}^{N}\, X_n 
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}\label{4.2.2}
Y(Z_Y)\,=\, \sum\limits_{n=0}^{N}\, ch_n\, H_n(Z_Y) \, =\, \sum\limits_{n=0}^{N}\, Y_n 
\end{equation}

Variance associated with each Hermite-polynomial part in Hermite-Series of X and Y is given in two equations below as
\begin{equation}\label{4.2.3}
Var_{X,n}\,=\, E \left[ {X_n}^2\right]\,=\, {bh_n}^2 E \left[ {H_n(Z_X)}^2\right]\,=\, {bh_n}^2 n! 
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}\label{4.2.4}
Var_{Y,n}\,=\, E \left[ {Y_n}^2\right]\,=\, {ch_n}^2 E \left[ {H_n(Z_Y)}^2\right]\,=\, {ch_n}^2 n! 
\end{equation}

Covariance between each order of Hermite polynomials in  Hermite-Series of two random variables X and Y  is given in two equations below as
\begin{equation}\label{4.2.5}
Cov_{XY,n}\,=\, E \left[ X_n \, Y_n\right]\,=\, b_n\, ch_n\, E \left[ H_n(Z_X) \, H_n(Z_Y) \right]\,=\, bh_n \, ch_n \, \rho_{XY,n} n! 
\end{equation}

And finally total Variances and Covariances between X nad Y are given as follows 
\begin{equation}\label{4.2.6}
Var[Y\,]=\,\sum\limits_{n=1}^{N}\,Var_{Y,n}\, 
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}\label{4.2.7}
Var[X\,]=\,\sum\limits_{n=1.}^{N}\,Var_{X,n}\, 
\end{equation}


\begin{equation}\label{4.2.8}
Cov_{XY}\,=\,\sum\limits_{n=1}^{N}\,Cov_{XY,n}\, 
\end{equation}





Conditional Expectation of a Non-Gaussian Random variable Y given Another Correlated Non-Gaussian Random variable X

In this section, we describe conditional expectation of a non-Gaussian random variable Y given the value of another correlated non-Gaussian random variable X. Orthogonality property of Hermite polynomials and decomposition of variance into orthogonal parts means that conditional expectation of a random variable Y with respect to other random variables X can be divided into separate covariance/correlation based relationships between Hermite polynomials of the same order of random variables X and Y . Further implying that the total conditional expectation of a random variable Y based on another correlated random variable X can be described by sum of separate conditional expectations of each Hermite polynomial of random variable Y with respect to Hermite polynomials of the same order of the random variable X . 

The conditional expectation of nth Hermite polynomial based term (with coefficient \(ch_n\)) of the random variable Y given nth polynomial term of X is calculated in terms of covariance \(Cov_{XY,n}\) between nth polynomials of both random variables X and Y and variance \(Var_{X,n}\) of the nth Hermite polynomial of explanatory variable X as 

\begin{equation}\label{4.3.1}
E[Y_n\,|\, X_n]\,= \, E[\,\,ch_n\,H_n(Z_Y)\,|\,bh_n.\,H_n(Z_X)]\, =\,\frac{Cov_{XY,n}}{Var_{X,n}}\,bh_n \, H_n(Z_X) 
\end{equation}

while expectation of variance associated with the conditional expectation \(E[Y_n\,|\, X_n]\,\) is given as

\begin{align}\label{4.3.2}
 & Var[Y_n\,|\, X_n]\,= Var[\,\,ch_n \, H_n(Z_Y)\,|\,bh_n\,H_n(Z_X)]\,=\,Var_{Y,n}\,-\frac{{Cov_{XY,n}}^2}{Var_{X,n}}\,\\ &=(1\,-\, {\rho_{XY,n}}^2) \, Var_{Y,n}\,\, =(1\,-\, {\rho_{XY,n}}^2) \, {ch_n}^2\, n! \,
\end{align}

We can write sum of conditional expectations of all Hermite polynomials of target variable Y with respect to explanatory variable as

\begin{equation}\label{4.3.4}
 \sum\limits_{n=1}^{N} E[\,\,ch_n \, H_n(Z_Y),|\,bh_n\,H_n(Z_X)]\,=\,\sum\limits_{n=1}^{N}\frac{Cov_{XY,n}}{Var_{X,n}}\,\,bh_n \, H_n(Z_X) 
 \end{equation}

But we know that conditional expectation of Y is equal to sum of conditional expectations of each orthogonal polynomial of Y including the zeroth hermite polynomial which is the unconditional mean of Y itself, so we can write

\begin{equation}\label{4.3.5}
E[Y \, | \, X]=\mu_Y+ \sum\limits_{n=1}^{N} E[\,\,ch_n \, H_n(Z_Y)\, | \,\,bh_n \,H_n(Z_X) ]
\end{equation}

which means that 

\begin{equation}\label{4.3.6}
E[Y\, | \, X]=\mu_Y+\,\sum\limits_{n=1}^{N}\frac{Cov_{XY,n}}{Var_{X,n}}\,\,bh_n \, H_n(Z_X) 
\end{equation}

which is equivalent to following equation (Please note this following equation as we will use its multivariate version in next post and in further analytics)

\begin{equation}
E[Y\, | \, X]=\mu_Y+\,\sum\limits_{n=1}^{N} ch_n\,\,\rho_{XY,n} \, H_n(Z_X) 
\end{equation}

and expected value of total variance, which is variance associated with part of random variable Y that has not been explained by conditioning on random variable X, associated with conditional expectation of Y given X \(E[Y\, | \, X]\) is given as

\begin{align}\label{4.3.7}
& Var[Y\, | \, X]=\,\sum\limits_{n=1}^{N}\,Var_{Y,n}\,-\frac{{Cov_{XY,n}}^2}{Var_{X,n}}\, =\,\sum\limits_{n=1}^{N}\,(1\,-\, {\rho_{XY,n}}^2) \, Var_{Y,n}\,\, \\ &= \,\,\sum\limits_{n=1}^{N}\,(1\,-\, {\rho_{XY,n}}^2) \, {ch_n}^2\, n!\, 
\end{align}
In the above equation, We have given the formulas for conditional expected value and the expected value of variance of conditional expectation of the random variable Y given random variable X.

Calculation of Conditional Expectation of Y Given K Correlated Random Variables  \(X_1, X_2, X_3, \ldots \,,X_K\) 


 Let us suppose, there are K conditioning variables  K random variables \(X_1, X_2, X_3, \ldots \,,X_K\)  and we want to find the conditional expectation of Y given all of \(X_1, X_2, X_3, \ldots \,,X_K\) . In such a case effective correlations could be found by standardized regression on correlations.

Suppose we have a Hermite-series of random variable Y given as

\[Y(Z_y)\,=\,\sum\limits_{n=0}^{N}\,ah_n\,H_n(Z_y)\]

and Hermite-Series of each of \(X_1, X_2, X_3, \ldots \,,X_K\) is given as

\[X_k(Z_{x_k})\,=\,\sum\limits_{n=0}^{N}\,bh_{k,n}\,H_n(Z_{x_k})\]

We define the correlation matrix of nth hermite polynomial of K explaining  random variables \(X_1, X_2, X_3, \ldots \,,X_K\) as 
\[\textbf{R}_n \,=\,

         \rho_{X_1 X_1,n} & \rho_{X_2 X_1,n} & \cdots & \rho_{X_K X_1,n}\\
         \rho_{X_1 X_2,n} & \rho_{X_2 X_2,n} & \cdots & \rho_{X_K X_2,n}\\ 
         \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots\\ 
         \rho_{X_1 X_K,n} & \rho_{X_2 X_K,n} & \cdots & \rho_{X_K X_K,n} 
     \end{bmatrix}
\]

 \[\textbf{r}_{XY,n}\,=\begin{bmatrix}
          \rho_{X_1 Y,n} \\
 \rho_{X_2 Y,n}  \\ \cdots \\ \rho_{X_K Y,n}
     \end{bmatrix}\]

Then vector of effective correlation coefficients \(\beta_{n,k}\)  associated with kth random variable \(X_k\) and nth hermite polynomial that explains the nth hermite polynomial of Y is given as

\[
\begin{bmatrix}
          \beta_{n,1} \\
 \beta_{n,2}  \\ \cdots \\ \beta_{n_K}
     \end{bmatrix}
=
{\begin{bmatrix}
         \rho_{X_1 X_1,n} & \rho_{X_2 X_1,n} & \cdots & \rho_{X_K X_1,n}\\

         \rho_{X_1 X_K,n} & \rho_{X_2 X_K,n} & \cdots & \rho_{X_K X_K,n} 
     \end{bmatrix}}^{-1}
\,
\begin{bmatrix}
          \rho_{X_1 Y,n} \\
 \rho_{X_2 Y,n}  \\ \cdots \\ \rho_{X_K Y,n}
     \end{bmatrix}
\]

The above equation is equation of standardized regression that is done on correlations. Inverse of variance-covariance matrix of regressors is replaced by inverse of correlation matrix of regressors \({\textbf{R}_n}^{-1} \). And covariance vector between regressand and regressors is replaced by the respective correlation vector \(\textbf{r}_{XY,n}\) making the standardized regression equation as 

\[ \boldsymbol{\beta}_n ={\textbf{R}_n}^{-1} \, \textbf{r}_{XY,n}\]

Again \(\beta_{n,K}\) is the effective correlation between nth hermite polynomial of Y and nth Hermite polynomial of \(X_k\) and this correlation would be used in later analytics instead of the raw hermite correlations.

The problem to solve is to fiund the conditional density of sum \(Y(T_2)\,=\,X_1(T_2)\,+\,X_2(T_2)\,+X_3(T_2)\,+X_4(T_2)\)  given the arbitrarily chosen values of \(X1(T_1), X2(T_1), X3(T_1) \,\text{and}\,\, X4(T_1)\). I then did an independent monte carlo simulation of three densities from chosen values of \(X1(T_1), X2(T_1), X3(T_1) \,\text{and}\,\, X4(T_1)\). and continued it for time \(T2-T1\)(This gave me the true monte carlo conditional distribution of \(Y(T_2)\,=\,X_1(T_2)\,+\,X_2(T_2)\,+X_3(T_2)\,+X_4(T_2)\)). I overlaid the densities of analytic solution of density given as \(P(Y(T_2)|\,X_1(T_1)=x_1\,,\,X_2(T_1)=x_2\,,X_3(T_1)=x_3\,,X_4(T_1)=x_4) \) and the monte carlo derived density of Y. Even though I used four explaining variables, the logic is for arbitrary N variables. Just change NDim=4 to appropriate N and supply SDE parameters.

Basically \(Y(T_2)\,=\,X_1(T_2)\,+\,X_2(T_2)\,+X_3(T_2)+X_4(T_2)\)  and we are conditioning as

\[P(Y(T_2)|\,X_1(T_1)=x_1\,,\,X_2(T_1)=x_2\,,X_3(T_1)=x_3\,,X_4(T_1)=x_4) \]

The algorithm is very fast with almost zero training time ( less than a second for this data) as compared to machine learning that may take days or even weeks to properly train the network and the output of algorithm is better than what is mostly found out of  neural networks after several hundred hours of training. In our algorithm most time is used in monte carlo simulations of data that is required for generation of variables and for finding the reference simulation density(taken as true density for comparison) and not in training of the parameters.

Our algorithm has just knowledge of joint (random) data of \(Y(T_2)\) and  \(X1(T_1), X2(T_1), X3(T_1) \,\text{and}\,\, X4(T_1)\). And conditional value of density \(P(Y(T_2)|\,X_1(T_1)=x_1\,,\,X_2(T_1)=x_2\,,X_3(T_1)=x_3\,,X_4(T_1)=x_4) \) is calculated by our algorithm.

I also want to notice that our predicted variable Y is sum of N variables in the above example so its variance and higher moments continue to increase with increasing predictors, however, when we are trying to explain just one fixed variable Y with N explaining variables correlated with Y, the predicted conditional density would continue to become sharper with decreasing variance.
