In this post I try to heuristicaly relate standard deviations calculus method of evolution of density of a stochastic differential equation to Fokker Planck equation. For the sake of simplicity I do not consider drift and only consider a diffusion with state dependent volatility and then heuristically relate new method to the Fokker Planck PDE. Let us consider the diffusion

[$]\text{dX}(t)=\sigma (X(t))\text{dz}(t) [$]  Equation 1.

The corresponding Fokker Planck goes as

[$]\frac{\partial p}{\partial t}=.5\frac{\partial ^2\left(\sigma ^2(x)p\right)}{\partial x^2}[$]

Expanding the derivative on the right, we get

[$]\frac{\partial p}{\partial t}=.5\sigma ^2(x)\frac{\partial ^2p}{\partial x^2}+2\sigma (x)\frac{\partial \sigma (x)}{\partial x}\frac{\partial p}{\partial x}+(\left(\frac{\partial \sigma (x)}{\partial x}\right)^2+\sigma (x)\frac{\partial ^2\sigma (x)}{\partial x^2})p[$]          Equation (2)

In our method, we take local values of volatility [$]\text{$\sigma $(x)}[$]  in each subdivision. We start with the highest derivative which is the second order derivative in our case and find its local solution as a brownian motion density which is again scaled by local value of volatility  [$]\text{$\sigma $(x)}[$]    Let us write this solution as f(x) which indicates normal as brownian motion density here. Within each subdivision, the density will evolve according to normal brownian motion evolution scaled by local value of volatility. We will take expectations of other terms on right hand side with respect to This brownian motion density.

Taking the second term on right hand side of equation 2. Since we divide the density into large number of small subdivisions to find a local solution in each of them, we have to write for the first derivative of probability within each subdivision with boundaries [$]x_a[$] and  [$]x_b[$]

[$]\frac{\partial p\left(x_b\right)}{\partial x}-\frac{\partial p\left(x_a\right)}{\partial x}=\frac{\partial f\left(x_b\right)}{\partial x}-\frac{\partial f\left(x_a\right)}{\partial x}=\int_{x_a}^{x_b} \frac{\partial ^2f(x)}{\partial x^2} \, dx[$]  Equation (3)

Taking the last term and expanding further

[$]\int_{x_a}^{x_b} \frac{\partial ^2f(x)}{\partial x^2} \, dx=\int _{x_a}^{x_b}\left.(\left.\frac{\partial ^2f(x)}{\partial x^2}\right/f(x) \right)f(x)dx=E [\text{H2}(x)][$] Equation (4)

where the last term denotes expectation of second hermite polynomial over the subdivision heuristically recognized as expectation of the ratio of the second derivative of the density with respect to the density itself within the subdivision boundaries. I have written equations without normalizations and related coefficients.

Now considering the last term on right hand side of equation 2, we have for probability mass within each subdivision with boundaries [$]x_a[$] and  [$]x_b[$]

[$]p\left(x_b\right)-p\left(x_a\right)=f\left(x_b\right)-f\left(x_a\right)=\int_{x_a}^{x_b} \frac{\partial f(x)}{\partial x} \, dx[$]  Equation(5)
Taking the last term and expanding further
[$]\int_{x_a}^{x_b} \frac{\partial f(x)}{\partial x} \, dx=\int _{x_a}^{x_b}\left.(\left.\frac{\partial f(x)}{\partial x}\right/f(x) \right)f(x)dx=E [\text{H1}(x)][$]   Equation(6)

where the last term denotes expectation of first order hermite polynomial over the subdivision heuristically recognized as expectation of the ratio of the first derivative of the density of brownain motion with respect to the density itself within the subdivision boundaries.

Our method now heuristically goes like this. We start with the highest derivative which is the second order derivative in our case and find its local solution as a brownian motion density scaled by local value of variance [$]\text{$\sigma $(x)}[$]

We expand the density on standard deviations just like normal brownian motion density scaled locally in each subdivision by the coefficient of the second order derivative. Then we add the following terms. The first term goes as 

[$]2\sigma \left(x\left(t_0\right)\right)\frac{\partial \sigma \left(x\left(t_0\right)\right)}{\partial x}\int _{t_0}^{t_1}\frac{\partial p}{\partial x}dt[$]
[$]=2\sigma \left(x\left(t_0\right)\right)\frac{\partial \sigma \left(x\left(t_0\right)\right)}{\partial x}\int _{t_0}^{t_1}\int _{x_a}^{x_b}(\left.\frac{\partial ^2f(x)}{\partial x^2}\right/f(x) )f(x)dxdt[$]
[$]=2\sigma \left(x\left(t_0\right)\right)\frac{\partial \sigma \left(x\left(t_0\right)\right)}{\partial x}\int _{t_0}^{t_1}E[\text{H2}(x)]dt[$]  Equation(7)

The second term we add goes as

[$](\left(\frac{\partial \sigma \left(x\left(t_0\right)\right)}{\partial x}\right){}^2+\sigma \left(x\left(t_0\right)\right)\frac{\partial ^2\sigma \left(x\left(t_0\right)\right)}{\partial x^2})\int_{t_0}^{t_1} p \, dt[$]
[$]=(\left(\frac{\partial \sigma \left(x\left(t_0\right)\right)}{\partial x}\right){}^2+\sigma \left(x\left(t_0\right)\right)\frac{\partial ^2\sigma \left(x\left(t_0\right)\right)}{\partial x^2})\int _{t_0}^{t_1}\int _{x_a}^{x_b}(\left.\frac{\partial f(x)}{\partial x}\right/f(x) )f(x)dxdt[$]
[$]=(\left(\frac{\partial \sigma \left(x\left(t_0\right)\right)}{\partial x}\right){}^2+\sigma \left(x\left(t_0\right)\right)\frac{\partial ^2\sigma \left(x\left(t_0\right)\right)}{\partial x^2})\int _{t_0}^{t_1}E[\text{H1}(x)]dt[$]   Equation(8)

Sorry for ambiguity but Here I want to indicate that in equation 8(and similarly for equation 7), instead of integral 
[$]\int _{t_0}^{t_1}E[\text{H1}(x)]dt[$]
I have used the following solution
[$]E[\text{H1}(x(t_1))]-E[\text{H1}(x(t_0))][$]

My constants in the programs I uploaded were based on difference in the expectations of hermite polynomials associated with brownian motion for evolution from each simulation interval starting time to simulation interval end time associated with each probability mass subdivision.

I will like to mention that this second term as given by equation (8) was missing from the solution of CEV density program I posted on this thread earlier and that is why we probably had to take very small steps to maintain accuracy.

I do not know if I had extended heuristics too far in the previous post in search for the right solution but we all know that for a general parabolic equation of the Fokker Planck Form, the general solution that exists for constant coefficients is well known. 

According to book by Andrei D. Polyanin and Vladimir E. Nazaiknskii p. 284-285, for constant coefficients PDE of the form

[$]\frac{\partial p}{\partial t}=A\frac{\partial ^2p}{\partial x^2}+B\frac{\partial p}{\partial x}+C p[$]

The above constant coefficients PDE takes the fundamental solution

[$]p(x,t)=\frac{1}{2\sqrt{\pi  A t}}\text{Exp}\left[-\frac{(x + B t) }{4 A t }+C t\right][$]

We could replace state dependent coefficients instead of constant coefficients and the solution would be applicable in the immediate neighborhood where we calculated the values of state dependent coefficients.

[$]A=.5\sigma ^2(x)[$]
[$]B=2\sigma (x)\frac{\partial \sigma (x)}{\partial x}[$]
[$]C=(\left(\frac{\partial \sigma (x)}{\partial x}\right)^2+\sigma (x)\frac{\partial ^2\sigma (x)}{\partial x^2})[$]

The Hermite polynomials are given by the exponential generating function
[$]\text{Exp}\left[z t -.5 t^2\right]=\sum _{n=0}^{\infty } \text{He}_n(z) \frac{t^n}{n!}[$]

By comparison with the exponential generating function, Girsanov exponential finds a direct expansion in terms of hermite polynomials.
Consider a general SDE with state dependent drift.

[$]\text{dX}=\mu (x) \text{dt} + \sigma (x) \text{dz}(t)[$]

The Girsanov exponential associated with the SDE is given as

[$]G(X)=\text{Exp}\left[\frac{\mu (x)}{\sigma (x)}\text{dz}(t)-.5\left(\frac{\mu (x)}{\sigma (x)}\right)^2\text{dt}\right][$]

and can be directly expanded everywhere locally in the form of hermite polynomials. This is the right way in order to get precise dynamics when drift is added to the SDE. In order to get the density of the above SDE with state dependent drift, when we expand the product of density without drift and Girsanov exponential, we get the original density without drift(zero order polynomial) and local expectations of higher order hermite polynomials based on Girsanov exponential.

Here is a simple description of method of iterated integrals as I have written in my paper coming in a day.

Ordinary differential equations define a differential relationship which is usually a low order derivative between the independent variable and the dependent variable. This differential relationship is usually a function that can possibly take a large number of derivatives with respect to both variables. In an initial value problem, in general, when we take step in independent variable, this differential relationship has to be integrated along the independent variable to find the change in the dependent variable and a perfect integration would require the knowledge of higher total derivatives of differential function with respect to independent variable. Previously, in context of SDEs and ODEs there were no general ways to relate higher total derivatives of the differential function to the variation of the independent variable and many times order of the differential function/equation would limit the useful knowledge when integrating the differential function/equation with respect to the independent variable. In  method of iterated integrals, we add consecutive terms in which we differentiate the previous total derivative with respect to dependent variable and convert  it to higher derivative with respect to the independent variable through chain rule derivative usually described by the differential function/equation itself. Since we do not include derivatives of the differential function with respect to independent variable,, by saying ‘total derivative’, the purpose is to merely emphasize the indirect dependence of differential equation/function on independent variable through chain rule linking the derivatives of differential equation due to dependent variable into variation due to independent variable. In fact, mostly independent variable could be directly integrated in the differential equation/function and we would not need derivatives of differential equation with respect to independent variable. However if this direct integration with respect to independent variable could not be carried out, we would in fact need total derivatives in the formal sense to integrate the differential function. 
Many people would like to think of method of iterated integrals as a dependent variable analog of Taylor series where moments of variation in the independent variable are mapped on the change in dependent variable through appropriately calculated higher derivatives of the differential function. Each iterated integral term in the series calculates the correction of one higher order derivative to the integral of differential function as the independent variable moves from one point to another.

Solution to Second Order ODEs

We find the solution to General second order Ordinary Differential Equations given as
[$]\frac{d^2y}{dt^2}=f\left(t,y,y'\right)[$],   [$]t\geq 0[$],        [$]y\left(t_0\right)=y_0[$],    [$]y'\left(t_0\right)=y'_0[$]
Equation (18)
We suppose that function f in the equation above is sufficiently well behaved and satisfies the usual regularity conditions.
Integrating Equation (18), we get the solution
[$]y(t)=y\left(t_0\right)+y'\left(t_0\right)\left(t-t_0\right)[$]
[$]+\int _{t_0}^t\int _{t_0}^sf\left(\tau ,y(\tau ),y'(\tau )\right)d\tau ds[$] 
Equation (19)
The differential function in the second order ODE takes three arguments in general. In a direct integration, the independent variable would likely get integrated but we have to take partial derivatives of differeential function with respect to both dependent arguments and convert them to indirect derivatives with respect to independent variable using the chain rule and given initial values of the arguments and the differential function/equation itself. We would call the sum of these indirect derivatives on nth order as indirect total derivative of (that) nth order order.

Here we expand the double integral in Equation (19) by terms required to take one indirect total derivative

Example 4
Consider the 2nd order initial value problem given as
