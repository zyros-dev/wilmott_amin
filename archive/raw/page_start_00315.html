\[ y^{2}(t)=t+\tfrac{1}{3}t^3 \]

\[ y^{3}(t)=t +\tfrac{1}{3}t^3 +\tfrac{2}{15}t^5+\tfrac{1}{63}t^7 \]
and the differences get larger from there...

The primary question was what do you wish to state about (7). 
Secondary question is differentiability. If you look at the right hand side (7) and apply differentiation with respect to y then you will get 2nd derivative of f with respect y. Expending your approach by n times the derivative order looks like should be increased. 
Equation(7) is another formal solution to Equation(1) which is equivalent to formal solution as stated in Equation(2). However the first two terms in the formal solution in Equation (7) can be easily evaluated as they are evaluated at y(t0) and the last third term has to be considered in the formal sense. We can find infinite number of formal solutions equivalent to Equation(2) and equivalent to each other where n number of terms (n is an arbitrary integer) have been evaluated at y(t0) and final n+1 th term has to be understood in formal sense and could not be evaluated in general. We hope to choose a formal solution in which enough number of terms have been evaluated at y(t0) so that the last term which is considered in formal sense and cannot be evaluated has negligible value. Of course, like many other series, this may always not be the case. And of course, some mathematical conditions would need to apply for all solutions to be equivalent but I am sure most mathematicians can easily work that out since most of the ideas are based on simple calculus.

However, since the method finds out series solution in natural derivatives of function y(t), we can many time look at the series and be able to find out what function it is representing and it could lead us to closed form solution of the problem. This is a new method and I am sure there is a lot of work that needs to be done to work out everything in complete detail. For example there could be some better ways of 'finding the primitive' in many specialized cases but integration is a natural generic choice that would mostly work. I have tried to make enough effort by sharing mathematica programs so other friends could easily try it on ODEs of their choice. It would have been almost impossible to work out these iterated integrals by hand in most of the practical cases.

Regarding your secondary question. before I differentiate the function each time, I multiply the previous result first with the dy/dt=f(y,t) and that is why the function has to be just once differentiable. I am sure if you do an example  ODE dy/dt=y, you will very easily find out.
"Equation(7) is another formal solution to Equation(1) which is equivalent to formal solution as stated in Equation(2)" ///
It is not right. Formally it should be sounded as Eq (1) and Eq(2) are equivalent. That means that each solution (1) is a solution (2) and inverse. When you start with(1) it means that you assume that (1) has a solution otherwise it does not make sense. Then starting from (2) assuming that there exists a solution of (2) you can arrive at (1), ie the eq (1) and (2) are equivalent given your assumptions. You should also make an assumption on growth of the f in y. Otherwise solution can reach infinity during a finite time interval. Actually you can do this for arbitrary finite iteration n > 1 too. If you think that having n-th iterative expression you can drop the last term that might be also true you need to prove that given your assumption on f ( t , y ) the last term will tends to zero.
