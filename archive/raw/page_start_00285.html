I have re-written the introduction to method of iterated integrals and tried to make it more accessible. Earlier exposition was not very good but in the revision, I am sure that people would easily understand most concepts. Here is the new simpler version of the explanation of the basics of the method. It is probably not a formal proof but I am sure a formal proof can easily be derived from these lines.

2. Introduction to the Method of Iterated Integrals
 
To start the discussion, we try to find the solution to General initial value problem associated with first order Ordinary Differential Equations given as
[$]\frac{dy}{dt}=f(t,y)[$],  [$]t\geq 0[$], [$]y\left(t_0\right)=y_0[$] Equation(1)

Integrating Equation (1), we get the formal solution to the initial value problem as
[$]y(t)=y\left(t_0\right)+\int_{t_0}^t f(\tau ,y(\tau )) \, d\tau[$]  Equation(2)

If only we could exactly solve the formal integral in equation (2), we would find our desired solution. Obviously [$]f(\tau ,y(\tau ))[$]cannot in general be directly and analytically integrated in the above integral.
However we can write differential function in the formal integral in equation (2) as a function of initial value of  [$]y\left(t_0\right)[$] and a formal integral of appropriate derivative of the differential function with respect to y so that sum of both terms is equated with differential function in formal integral in equation (2). We can for example write as
[$]f(\tau ,y(\tau ))=f\left(\tau ,y\left(t_0\right)\right)+\int _{t_0}^{\tau }\frac{\partial }{\partial y}[f(\tau ,y(s))]\frac{dy(s)}{ds}ds[$] Equation(3)
[$]f(\tau ,y(\tau ))=f\left(\tau ,y\left(t_0\right)\right)+\int _{t_0}^{\tau }\frac{\partial }{\partial y}[f(\tau ,y(s))]f(s,y(s))ds[$]  Equation(4)
Where we have used equation (1) with equation (3) to get equation (4).

Equation (2) can now be written after substituting Equation(4) in it as
[$]y(t)=y\left(t_0\right)+\int_{t_0}^t f\left(\tau ,y\left(t_0\right)\right) \, d\tau +\int _{t_0}^t\int _{t_0}^{\tau }\frac{\partial }{\partial y}[f(\tau ,y(s))]f(s,y(s))dsd\tau[$] Equation(5)

In Equation(5), we notice that first order integral in second term could mostly be easily evaluated while the second order integral in third term has to be taken in formal sense and this second order integral could not, in general, be easily evaluated.
However the term inside the second order formal integral in equation (5) can again similarly be written in terms of initial values [$]y\left(t_0\right)[$] and an appropriate formal integral as 
[$]\frac{\partial }{\partial y}[f(\tau ,y(s))]f(s,y(s))=\frac{\partial }{\partial y}\left[f\left(\tau ,y\left(t_0\right)\right)\right]f\left(s,y\left(t_0\right)\right)[$]
[$]+\int _{t_0}^s\frac{\partial }{\partial y}\left[\frac{\partial }{\partial y}[f(\tau ,y(v))]f(s,y(v))\right]\frac{dy(v)}{dv}dv[$]
[$]=\frac{\partial }{\partial y}\left[f\left(\tau ,y\left(t_0\right)\right)\right]f\left(s,y\left(t_0\right)\right)+\int _{t_0}^s\frac{\partial }{\partial y}\left[\frac{\partial }{\partial y}[f(\tau ,y(v))]f(s,y(v))\right]f(v,y(v))dv[$]  Equation(6)

Substituting Equation(6) in the second order formal integral in equation (5), we can write as
[$]y(t)=y\left(t_0\right)+\int_{t_0}^t f\left(\tau ,y\left(t_0\right)\right) \, d\tau +\int _{t_0}^t\int _{t_0}^{\tau }\frac{\partial }{\partial y}\left[f\left(\tau ,y\left(t_0\right)\right)\right]f\left(s,y\left(t_0\right)\right)dsd\tau[$]
[$]+\int _{t_0}^t\int _{t_0}^{\tau }\int _{t_0}^s\left(\frac{\partial }{\partial y}\left[\frac{\partial }{\partial y}[f(\tau ,y(v))]f(s,y(v))\right]f(v,y(v))\right)dvdsd\tau[$] Equation (7)

In the equation (7), the first order and second order integrals can easily be evaluated at initial values [$]y\left(t_0\right)[$]while the third integral has to be taken in the formal sense.
We can continue to expand the highest order formal integral in form of iterated integral terms evaluated at  [$]y\left(t_0\right)[$]and one order higher formal integral and continue to add further terms in our series solution.
It is interesting to notice intuitively how the upper limits of the inside integrals go to time in each outer integral so as the higher indirect derivative in inner integrals make a contribution to lower indirect derivatives only till running time in successive outer integral.

Here is "my" approach to solving odes...

Write the system as:
\[ \frac{dy(t)}{dt}=\epsilon f(t,y(t)),\quad y(t_0)=y_0, \]
The parameter epsilon is in there as a formal expansion parameter, but this can easily be seen to be a small time expansion by scaling the interval to [0,1].

Now, introduce the formal series solution:
\[ y(t)=y_0+\epsilon y_1(t)+\epsilon^2y_2(t)+\epsilon^3y_3(t)+\ldots, \]
Insert this into the left and right sides of the ode, and perform a Taylor expansion in epsilon and match order-by-order.

The first non-trivial order gives:
\[ y_1(t)=\int_{t_0}^t ds f(s,y(t_0)), \]
the next:
\[ y_2(t)=\int_{t_0}^t ds \frac{\partial f}{\partial y}(s,y(t_0)) y_1(s)=\int_{t_0}^t ds \int_{t_0}^s du  \frac{\partial f}{\partial y}(s,y(t_0)) f(u,y(t_0)) \]
and so on.

Now:
1. This approach is definitely not new (wasn't Newton doing something like this when he `invented' calculus?).
2. This approach is seldom useful -- in fact, I see perturbation expansions like this more often in the introduction of books dedicated to giving better approaches.
So definitely not a breakthrough.

But:
1. The first two orders in the expansion given above are the same as the `iterated integrals' approach.
2. I can reproduce all the expansions (to all orders given) of first order ode examples using this expansion (with a straightforward extension to systems of equations) -- power series or not.
3. The results from the few higher-order examples I looked at were also replicated by naive conversion into a system of first-order equations.

Obviously I can't claim that iterated integrals is just a simple perturbation expansion in disguise (because I still do not understand the presentation of ii), but I am still left wondering how/where it is making significant progress...
So it looks like this is a "perturbation expansion"? Shouldn't you research this Amin? It would be a waste of time to continue if it has all been done before.

However we can write differential function in the formal integral in equation (2) as a function of initial value of  [$]y\left(t_0\right)[$] and a formal integral of appropriate derivative of the differential function with respect to y so that sum of both terms is equated with differential function in formal integral in equation (2). We can for example write as
[$]f(\tau ,y(\tau ))=f\left(\tau ,y\left(t_0\right)\right)+\int _{t_0}^{\tau }\frac{\partial }{\partial y}[f(\tau ,y(s))]\frac{dy(s)}{ds}ds[$] Equation(3)
[$]f(\tau ,y(\tau ))=f\left(\tau ,y\left(t_0\right)\right)+\int _{t_0}^{\tau }\frac{\partial }{\partial y}[f(\tau ,y(s))]f(s,y(s))ds[$]  Equation(4)
Where we have used equation (1) with equation (3) to get equation (4).

Equation (2) can now be written after substituting Equation(4) in it as
[$]y(t)=y\left(t_0\right)+\int_{t_0}^t f\left(\tau ,y\left(t_0\right)\right) \, d\tau +\int _{t_0}^t\int _{t_0}^{\tau }\frac{\partial }{\partial y}[f(\tau ,y(s))]f(s,y(s))dsd\tau[$] Equation(5)

In Equation(5), we notice that first order integral in second term could mostly be easily evaluated while the second order integral in third term has to be taken in formal sense and this second order integral could not, in general, be easily evaluated.
However the term inside the second order formal integral in equation (5) can again similarly be written in terms of initial values [$]y\left(t_0\right)[$] and an appropriate formal integral as 
[$]\frac{\partial }{\partial y}[f(\tau ,y(s))]f(s,y(s))=\frac{\partial }{\partial y}\left[f\left(\tau ,y\left(t_0\right)\right)\right]f\left(s,y\left(t_0\right)\right)[$]
[$]+\int _{t_0}^s\frac{\partial }{\partial y}\left[\frac{\partial }{\partial y}[f(\tau ,y(v))]f(s,y(v))\right]\frac{dy(v)}{dv}dv[$]
[$]=\frac{\partial }{\partial y}\left[f\left(\tau ,y\left(t_0\right)\right)\right]f\left(s,y\left(t_0\right)\right)+\int _{t_0}^s\frac{\partial }{\partial y}\left[\frac{\partial }{\partial y}[f(\tau ,y(v))]f(s,y(v))\right]f(v,y(v))dv[$]  Equation(6)

Substituting Equation(6) in the second order formal integral in equation (5), we can write as
[$]y(t)=y\left(t_0\right)+\int_{t_0}^t f\left(\tau ,y\left(t_0\right)\right) \, d\tau +\int _{t_0}^t\int _{t_0}^{\tau }\frac{\partial }{\partial y}\left[f\left(\tau ,y\left(t_0\right)\right)\right]f\left(s,y\left(t_0\right)\right)dsd\tau[$]
[$]+\int _{t_0}^t\int _{t_0}^{\tau }\int _{t_0}^s\left(\frac{\partial }{\partial y}\left[\frac{\partial }{\partial y}[f(\tau ,y(v))]f(s,y(v))\right]f(v,y(v))\right)dvdsd\tau[$] Equation (7)

In the equation (7), the first order and second order integrals can easily be evaluated at initial values [$]y\left(t_0\right)[$]while the third integral has to be taken in the formal sense.
We can continue to expand the highest order formal integral in form of iterated integral terms evaluated at  [$]y\left(t_0\right)[$]and one order higher formal integral and continue to add further terms in our series solution.
It is interesting to notice intuitively how the upper limits of the inside integrals go to time in each outer integral so as the higher indirect derivative in inner integrals make a contribution to lower indirect derivatives only till running time in successive outer integral.

However the first two terms in the formal solution in Equation (7) can be easily evaluated as they are evaluated at y(t0) and the last third term has to be considered in the formal sense. We can find infinite number of formal solutions equivalent to Equation(2) and equivalent to each other where n number of terms (n is an arbitrary integer) have been evaluated at y(t0) and final n+1 th term has to be understood in formal sense and could not be evaluated in general. We hope to choose a formal solution in which enough number of terms have been evaluated at y(t0) so that the last term which is considered in formal sense and cannot be evaluated has negligible value. Of course, like many other series, this may always not be the case. And of course, some mathematical conditions would need to apply for all solutions to be equivalent but I am sure most mathematicians can easily work that out since most of the ideas are based on simple calculus.

However, since the method finds out series solution in natural derivatives of function y(t), we can many time look at the series and be able to find out what function it is representing and it could lead us to closed form solution of the problem. This is a new method and I am sure there is a lot of work that needs to be done to work out everything in complete detail. For example there could be some better ways of 'finding the primitive' in many specialized cases but integration is a natural generic choice that would mostly work.
