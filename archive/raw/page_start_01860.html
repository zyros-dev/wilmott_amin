The more I think about it, the more I realize that CDF is the most magical thing in probability theory. We earlier found that due to same CDF, the matrix columns of Z's underlying Z-series of a random variable's observations and the columns of underlying Z's of Z-series of its functions would be perfectly collinear. I think CDF has several other such magical properties.
In the expression for random variable [$]\, X_{t_1}(Z,Z_0) \,[$] given in Eq(7), we have two underlying standard normal variables, and both these Z's are independent of each other. 
If we want to reduce the dimensionality of the above expression from two dimensions to a single dimension, the only plausible way seems to do that along constant CDF lines in two dimensions.
However complicated the density of  [$]\, X_{t_1}(Z,Z_0) \,[$], the dynamics of underlying probability mass and associated CDF are very simple and almost linear due to two independent underlying standard normals.
Since both Z's are independent, we can define a single Z along tilted coordinates as below. This has been normalized to remain a standard normal.
[$] \, Z_1 \, = \, \frac{\sqrt{\, {Z_0}^2 \, + \, Z^2 \, }} { \, \sqrt{2} }\, [$]  Equation(1)

We also notice that in given form of Equation(7), we can plot [$]\, X_{t_1}(Z,Z_0) \,[$] on a square grid with [$]Z[$] and [$]Z_0[$] as its axes using bivariate change of probability/Jacobian similar to one dimensional change of probability we used for graphing one dimensional densities. But We do not need to draw a graph, what is important is that from knowledge of [$]Z[$] and [$]Z_0[$] we can easily find bivariate CDF anywhere and also the associated value of the desired variable [$]\, X_{t_1}(Z,Z_0) \,[$]. We notice that tilted straight lines of common constant bivariate CDF on [$]Z[$] and [$]Z_0[$] axis can easily be drawn and these are the ones that define CDF along our new single variable [$]Z_1[$] in Equation(1). I want to reduce dimensionality by averaging the value of [$]\, X_{t_1}(Z,Z_0) \,[$] along constant CDF lines on bivariate grid and assigning this CDF and associated averaged/integrated value of [$]\, X_{t_1} [$] to resulting underlying standard normal variable [$]Z_1[$] calculated in Equation(1). This can be very quickly done on a sufficiently fine square grid as we do not want complete graphing. Once we have an expression of CDF of [$]\,X_{t_1}(Z_1) \,=\, X_{t_1}(Z,Z_0) \,[$] as a function of single underlying normal random variable [$]Z_1[$], we can use the analytic methods we have previously used to construct the Z-series representation of the random variable. This analytic method, I am referring to is previously given in the post here : https://forum.wilmott.com/viewtopic.php?f=4&t=99702&start=1785#p875176
In the above method, we use the knowledge of CDF of a random variable to find its inner product with various hermite polynomials. Due to orthogonality, inner product with each hermite polynomial finds coefficients associated with given random variable and the particular hermite polynomial so that these coefficients can be used to construct a Hermite series or alternatively a Z-series representation of the target random variable which happens to be [$]\, X_{t_1}(Z,Z_0) \,[$] in our case in this example. Since all the calculations in this semi-analytic numerical method are explicit, I hope that it will work perfectly well and will be far better than finding the Z-series from implicit inversion from moments of the random variable.
