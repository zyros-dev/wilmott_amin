https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=4933283

Here is the abstract:

Abstract
We give a series representation of non-Gaussian continuous random variables in terms of a Taylor Series (Z-Series) in Standard Gaussian random variable or alternatively a series in Hermite polynomials (Hermite-Series) of Standard Gaussian random variable. We present simple numerical methods to find coefficients of these series given the density, data or moments of the non-Gaussian random variable. We also show how to do basic operations like addition, subtraction, and taking functions of non-Gaussian random variables using their Z-series. We present the concept of correlations between Hermite polynomials of Hermite-Series of two non-Gaussian random variables. These Hermite polynomial correlations are far more robust than the correlations between non-Gaussian random variables we presently see in literature. Then we present methods to calculate conditional expected values and distributions of a non-Gaussian random variable given the value or distribution of another correlated non-Gaussian random variable. We apply the conditional distribution knowledge to calculation of regression coefficients in a Hermite-Series regression. At the end, We apply the methods learnt to various applications in simulation of Stochastic differential equations, financial trading and hedging of securities.


Keywords: Non-Gaussian Continuous Random variables, Non-Gaussian Conditional Expectation, Correlations, and Hermite-Series Regression

Friends, I have been trying to improve my iterative optimization algorithm that has worked reasonably well for finding coefficients of the Z-series that match the moments. I was thinking that it would be nice if the program could be generalized and used for more difficult multidimensional optimization problems. And I made some interesting observations.
In general optimization theory states that step size in multiple dimensions for optimization purposes have to be chosen proportional to derivative of the objective function being minimized/maximized. And I was surprised to know that this works quite poorly when I tried it find coefficients of Z-series that minimize the objective function. I cannot say what the reason but I speculate that this choice of step size in proportion to derivatives of objective function continues to seek local minima and gets trapped there. 
I think it is very important to stay away from local minima and still keep improving the objective function. While working with optimization of coefficients of Z-series, I noticed that it is more important to keep the step size in most important dimension changing independently with small increments while making sure that step size in other directions changes inversely proportionally to derivatives of objective function with respect to those dimensions. This makes sure that optimization does not go into local minima and it also makes sure that changes in objective function with respect to coefficients of other dimensions are smaller or roughly close to changes in objective function with respect to the most important dimension.
I really think that a large number of high dimensional optimization problems can be solved by iterative optimization like we have used for fitting Z-series to moments but one has to find the optimal ratio of step sizes and algorithm to optimally change this ratio along the optimization routine. If optimal ratio of step size could be known, iterative optimization can be quite successful for many such difficult high dimensional optimization problems. It requires some exploratory analysis of the problem and some experimentation with the problem to find optimal step size and how to alter this step size along the progress of optimization algorithm.

Friends, I was thinking of PDEs and I just realized that a lot of PDEs with continuous derivatives on a finite domain can be solved by expressing the underlying functions as Taylor series. With a finite domain, the Taylor series expansion would not blow at the boundaries. Of course, all functions with continuous derivatives cannot be easily captured by polynomials and normal density function is an obvious example. But most such functions can be captured by for example product of an intelligently chosen analytic function and a polynomial like we can multiply a Gaussian exponential function with  a polynomial with finite number of terms and then Taylor expand all of it and also take its derivatives and substitute them in a PDE.

In general optimization theory states that step size in multiple dimensions for optimization purposes have to be chosen proportional to derivative of the objective function being minimized/maximized. And I was surprised to know that this works quite poorly when I tried it find coefficients of Z-series that minimize the objective function. I think it is very important to stay away from local minima and still keep improving the objective function. While working with optimization of coefficients of Z-series, I noticed that it is more important to keep the step size in most important dimension changing independently with small increments while making sure that step size in other directions changes inversely proportionally to derivatives of objective function with respect to those dimensions. This makes sure that optimization does not go into local minima and it also makes sure that changes in objective function with respect to coefficients of other dimensions are smaller or roughly close to changes in objective function with respect to the most important dimension.

Friends, I believe that iterative optimization can be generalized to quite high dimensional optimization problems. All you need to so is to follow the following points.
1. Keep initial step size quite small.

2. Make the step size in each dimension inversely proportional to gradient of objective function with respect to that dimension meaning that change in objective function to perturbation in any dimension would be roughly of the same order not considering the sign of change in objective function. You do not have to impose this condition on every step. Since total optimization can take tens of thousands or hundreds of thousands iterations over all dimensions, you can apply the condition of step size being roughly inverse of the gradient of objective function after every hundred iteration steps (over all dimensions) or so. 

3. Again when the step size has been fixed according to consideration in previous two points, all you have to do is to add the step size in one direction and accept the iteration  if the objective function decreases and update the parameter in that dimension. If the objective function does not decrease by addition of step size in that dimension, you subtract the step size in that dimension and see if the objective function decreases. Again decrease in parameter is accepted if the objective function decreases with decrease in parameter in that particular dimension. If the objective function does not decrease in both directions by adding or subtracting the step size, we do not change the parameter of that direction and keep it at its old value from previous iteration. We repeat this process over all dimensions. And when there is no decrease in objective function after adding and subtracting the step sizes (that are roughly inversely proportional to gradient of objective function with respect to that dimension) in all dimensions, we cut the step size along all dimensions to .95 or .9 of the previous step size and optimization starts working again.

4. After repeated decrease of step size(as we cut step size when optimization in all dimensions fails with going step sizes at that time) a few multiple times, we try to see if the variables are almost in the same neighborhood as they were in previous decreases of step size, we can ascertain that we have reached close to global minimum and parameters are changing in the same neighborhood and we can switch to some gradient based strategy or possibly some Newton type strategy that can quickly take us to closest minimum since we expect that local minimum and global minimum there are the same.

Friends, I want to describe the method to find the conditional distribution of Y p(Y|X) when its orthogonal part is dependent on X. I am copying the section of my paper for the friends to get oriented and will explain the detailed method in next post.

Calculating Conditional Distribution of Random Variable Y When its Part Orthogonal to X is Dependent on Correlated Random Variable X

While calculating conditional expectation in previous section (\ref{sec4.5}), we have assumed that part of Y orthogonal to X is independent of X which is most likely not the case. For example consider an SDE-like dependence equation of a random variable

\begin{equation}\label{4.6.1}
 \,Y \,= \, \mu(X) \, + \, \sigma(X) \, W 
\end{equation}

where W would usually be a zero mean non-Gaussian random variable which is assumed to be independent of  \(\sigma(X) \,\). And \( \mu(X) \) and \( \sigma(X) \) are some functions of non-Gaussian random variable X. The first term \(\, \mu(X) \,\) should be perfectly captured by Hermite polynomial correlations in our conditional expectation equation but we see that orthogonal term \(\,\,\sigma(X) \, W\,\, \), in equation that describes Y, even though orthogonal to X  is not independent of X. So different realizations of X over its density should also affect and alter the conditional distribution of part of Y orthogonal to X and we would have to do more analytics to calculate the effect of X on orthogonal part of Y for a proper calculation of conditional distribution of Y.
In order to find the true distribution associated with univariate conditional expectation of a random variable Y given X, we again perform the de-correlation operation discussed in previous subsection (\ref{sec4.3}) on jointly observed values of data sample of X and Y, and then we can find that \(\tilde{Y}\) represents the Orthogonal part of Y that is not correlated to the variable X. 

This part of Y orthogonal to X can be written as an equation as

\begin{align}\label{4.6.2}
 & \tilde{Y} \,=\, Y\, -\, E\left[Y|X \right] \, \\ &=  \, Y\,-\,\,\sum\limits_{n=1}^{N}\,\frac{Cov_{XY,n}}{Var_{X,n}}\,bh_n \, H_n(Z_X) \\ \, &=  \, Y\,-\,\,\sum\limits_{n=1}^{N}\,\rho_{XY,n} \,ch_n \, H_n(Z_X)
\end{align}

We suppose that the knowledge of dependence structure of orthogonal term of Y on X is exogenously known and is given as \(\sigma(X)\). From section (\ref{sec2.5}), we already know that we can find a Z-series of \(\sigma(X(Z))\) through Taylor Series on Z-series of X.   

\begin{equation}\label{4.6.3}
\sigma(X(Z_X))=\sum\limits_{n=0}^{N}\, dh_n\, H_n(Z_X)
\end{equation}



Now we see that Z-series structure of \( \tilde{Y} \) with dependency structure included in equation is given as

\begin{equation}\label{4.6.6}
 \tilde{Y} \,=\, \, \sigma(X) \,\,W\,=\, \, \sigma(X) \,\, \sum\limits_{m=1}^{M}\, \tilde{ch}_m\, H_m(Z_W) \ \, 
 \end{equation}
 
The random variable \(\sum\limits_{m=1}^M \tilde{ch}_m  H_m(Z_W) \)  with as yet  undetermined coefficients  \(\tilde{ch}_m, (m=1,\ldots,M) \) is supposed to capture the distribution structure of non-Gaussian noise in the equation of part of Y that is orthogonal to X.

We use two different methods to estimate these coefficients depending upon if we are given data of joint observations of random variable X and Y or if we have knowledge of moments  of \(\tilde{Y}\) that is part of Y orthogonal to X.

 
Calculating Conditional Distribution of Part of Random Variable Y that is Orthogonal to but Dependent on Random Variable X When Numerical Data of X and Y is Available

Suppose. we have a total of P joint data observations of Y and X, with pth observation pair of both random variables is given as \(Y_p\) and \(X_p\). We first invert Hermite-Series equation of \(X_p\) to find \(Z_{X_p}\) and then use the following equation for each pth observation 

\begin{equation}\label{4.6.7}
 \tilde{Y_p} \,=  \, Y_p\,-\,\,\sum\limits_{n=1}^{N}\,\rho_{XY,n} \,ch_n \, H_n(Z_{X_p})
\end{equation}

As we described in previous section that we know the functional form of  \(\sigma(X)\) and we can easily Taylor expand it to find a Hermite-series given by equation (\ref{4.6.3}). For pth observation, this value of \(\sigma(X)\) can be represented as

\begin{equation}\label{4.6.8}
\sigma(X(Z_{X_p}))=\sum\limits_{n=0}^{N}\, dh_n\, H_n(Z_{X_p})
\end{equation}

since \(\tilde{Y_p} \,\) is given by equation(\ref{4.6.6}), for pth joint
observation of data, we would have
\begin{align}\label{4.6.9}
 &\tilde{Y_p} \,=\, \, \sigma(X_p) \,\,W_p\,\\
 & \,=\, \, \left(\sum\limits_{n=0}^{N}\, dh_n\, H_n(Z_{X_p}) \right) \,\,W_p\,
 \end{align}

 Since we have calculated \(\tilde{Y}_p\) for each of the pth joint observation \((p\,=\,1,\,\ldots,P)\) of Y and X in equation(\ref{4.6.7}) and we have also calculated Hermite Series of \(\sigma(X_p)\) in equation(\ref{4.6.8}), we could calculate the value of \(W_p\) associated with pth observation of as

 \begin{align}\label{4.6.10}
 &\, \,W_p\, \,=\, \, \frac{\tilde{Y_p}}{\sigma(X_p)} \,\\
 & \,=\, \,\, \, \frac{\tilde{Y_p}}{\left(\sum\limits_{n=0}^{N}\, dh_n\, H_n(Z_{X_p}) \right)} \,\\ 
 \end{align}

Depending upon the problem and application, it would be advisable to consider removing those values of data for which \(\left(\sum\limits_{n=0}^{N}\, dh_n\, H_n(Z_{X_p}) \right)\) is very small and where above equation nearly beomes singular.
After finding \(W_p\), we could use the method discussed in section(\ref{sec3.2}) by sorting data for \(W_p\,,(p\,=\,1,2,\ldots,P)\) and then regressing on Hermite polynomials calculated at discrete normal density grid associated with data size P and thus finding the coefficients of uni-variate Hermite series of W. Once, we have this series, represented as \(\, W\,=\,\sum\limits_{m=1}^{M}\, \tilde{ch}_m\, H_m(Z_W) \ \,\), we could represent conditional distribution of Y as


\begin{align}\label{4.6.11}
 & Y\, | \, X=\, E\left[Y|X \right]+\,\, \sigma(X(Z_{X_p})) \, W  \\
 &=\mu_Y+\,\sum\limits_{n=1}^{N}\,\rho_{XY,n} \, ch_n \, H_n(Z_X)+\,\, \left[ \sum\limits_{n=0}^{N}\, dh_n \, H_n(Z_X) \right] \, \left[\sum\limits_{m=1}^{M}\, \tilde{ch}_m\, H_m(Z_W)\right] 
 \end{align}
