@Cuch, I will try to explain Eq(3) and (6) and put them in context. I am writing the section 2 of the paper and then I will explain everything one equation evolves from the previous equation. If you have any further questions, I will love to explain.

2. Introduction to the Method of Iterated Integrals
 
To start the discussion, we try to find the solution to General initial value problem associated with first order Ordinary Differential Equations given as
[$]\frac{dy}{dt}=f(t,y),\text{   }t\geq 0,\text{    }y\left(t_0\right)=y_0[$]    Equation(1)
Integrating Equation (1), we get the solution to the initial value problem as 
[$]y(t)=y\left(t_0\right)+\int_{t_0}^t f(\tau ,y(\tau )) \, d\tau[$]
Equation (2)
If only we could exactly solve the integral as a function of  t, we would find our desired solution. Obviously [$]f(\tau ,y(\tau ))[$]
cannot in general be directly and analytically integrated in the above integral. However, the simplest approximation to initial value problem with Equation(2) could be found after substituting the initial values of y in the integral of differential function. This simple solution which is a crude approximation could be given as
[$]y(t)=y\left(t_0\right)+\int_{t_0}^t f\left(\tau ,y\left(t_0\right)\right) \, d\tau[$]
Equation(3)


But the differential function has dependence on [$]y(\tau )[$] which in turn depends on tau through the differential function itself. We could write the first indirect derivative of differential function with respect to independent variable as

[$]\frac{\partial }{\partial y}[f(\tau ,y(\tau ))]\frac{dy(\tau )}{dt}=\frac{\partial }{\partial y}[f(\tau ,y(\tau ))]f(\tau ,y(\tau ))[$]
Equation (4)


In fact, once we evaluate the Equation (3) at initial value, we could still find the precise value of the integral everywhere if we could exactly solve for the effect of first indirect derivative of t on the differential function. We could easily write the Equation (5) as
[$]y(t)=y\left(t_0\right)+\int_{t_0}^t f\left(\tau ,y\left(t_0\right)\right) \, d\tau +\int _{t_0}^t\int _{t_0}^{\tau }\frac{\partial }{\partial y}[f(\tau ,y(\tau ))]\frac{dy(s)}{dt}dsd\tau[$]
[$]y(t)=y\left(t_0\right)+\int_{t_0}^t f\left(\tau ,y\left(t_0\right)\right) \, d\tau +\int _{t_0}^t\int _{t_0}^{\tau }\frac{\partial }{\partial y}[f(\tau ,y(\tau ))]f(s,y(s))dsd\tau[$]
Equation(5)
In general, just like the original problem we cannot solve for the integrals in second term of Equation(5) exactly. However if we substitute initial values of variables in Equation(5), we have an approximation that is better than approximation in Equation(3) since we have added the effect of one indirect derivative evaluated at initial values to earlier approximation in Equation(3). Continuing to expand further on Equation(5), we get the new exact expansion in terms of higher indirect derivatives as 

[$]y(t)=y\left(t_0\right)+\int_{t_0}^t f\left(\tau ,y\left(t_0\right)\right) \, d\tau[$]
[$]+\int _{t_0}^t\int _{t_0}^{\tau }\frac{\partial }{\partial y}\left[f\left(\tau ,y\left(t_0\right)\right)\right]f\left(s,y\left(t_0\right)\right)dsd\tau[$]
[$]+\int _{t_0}^t\int _{t_0}^{\tau }\int _{t_0}^s\frac{\partial }{\partial y}\left[\frac{\partial }{\partial y}[f(\tau ,y(\tau ))]f(s,y(s))\right]f(v,y(v))dvdsd\tau[$]
Equation(6)

Again substituting initial values in Equation(6), we get a better approximation that we got in Equation(5).This expansion includes the effect of two indirect derivatives evaluated at initial values.

[$]y(t)=y\left(t_0\right)+\int_{t_0}^t f\left(\tau ,y\left(t_0\right)\right) \, d\tau[$]
[$]+\int _{t_0}^t\int _{t_0}^{\tau }\frac{\partial }{\partial y}\left[f\left(\tau ,y\left(t_0\right)\right)\right]f\left(s,y\left(t_0\right)\right)dsd\tau[$]
[$]\left.\left.+\int _{t_0}^t\int _{t_0}^{\tau }\int _{t_0}^s\frac{\partial }{\partial y}\left[\frac{\partial }{\partial y}\left[f\left(\tau ,y\left(y\left(t_0\right)\right)\right)\right]f\left(s,y\left(t_0\right)\right)\right)\right]f\left(v,y\left(t_0\right)\right)\right)dvdsd\tau[$]
Equation (6a)
We can continue to add further terms in our solution and evaluate those terms at the initial value and order of the method would continue to add precision.
if the independent variable is time, it is interesting to notice intuitively how the upper limits of the inside integrals go to time in each outer integral so as the higher indirect derivative in inner integrals make a contribution to lower indirect derivatives only till running time in successive outer integral.


The above is excerpt from the paper page(3-5). 
Intuitive Explanation:
We all know that Equation(2) is a formal solution. As Cuch pointed out Eq(3) is not right at all. It is a very crude approximation. In this equation, we have analytically integrated all the dependence on independent variable t and substituted initial values for the dependent variable. We have to realize since we analytically integrated with respect to independent variable t, the explicit dependence of differential equation/function w.r.t independent variable t has been totally taken care of. We do not need to take further partial derivatives w.r.t independent variable t. Now further dependence of differential equation/function on t is only through indirect derivative of t that is found after calculating the partial derivative with respect to y and converting into derivative with respect to t through chain rule. As in equation (5) we have calculated the first integral as in Equation (3) at the initial value and put all dependence of true solution on the only first indirect derivative that we have to consider(This derivative is again considered in the formal sense.)  So double integral in Equation (5) has to be considered in the formal sense.

In equation 6, we have calculated the formal double integral in Equation(5) by substituting initial values there and again putting all the dependence of true solution on one order higher indirect derivative(derivative of the previous indirect derivative of the differential equation/function converted into derivative with respect to independent variable through chain rule) which again has to be considered in the formal sense for the solution to be truly valid. So last triple integral in the equation (6) has to be considered in the formal sense.
Equation(6a) is a truncation of formal solution in Equation (6) to first three terms.

@Cuch, my method gives a perfect solution to the above example. You might have made some careless mistake but the method works very well on the example. Here is my solution.
Consider the 1st order initial value problem given as
[$]y'=f(\tau ,y(\tau ))=\frac{y}{\sqrt{\tau }},\text{    }y(0)=y_0[$],   [$]t_0=0[$]
From the general solution expansion given in Equation (6a), we calculate the coefficients of the first few terms as
[$]\int_{t_0}^t f\left(\tau ,y\left(t_0\right)\right) \, d\tau =\int_{t_0}^t \frac{y_0}{\sqrt{\tau }} \, d\tau =2\sqrt{t}y_0[$]
[$]\int _{t_0}^t\int _{t_0}^{\tau }\frac{\partial }{\partial y}\left[f\left(\tau ,y\left(t_0\right)\right)\right]f\left(s,y\left(t_0\right)\right)dsd\tau[$]
[$]=\int _{t_0}^t\int _{t_0}^{\tau }\frac{1}{\sqrt{\tau }}\frac{y_0}{\sqrt{s}}dsd\tau[$][$]=2t y_0[$]
[$]\left.\left.\int _{t_0}^t\int _{t_0}^{\tau }\int _{t_0}^s\frac{\partial }{\partial y}\left[\frac{\partial }{\partial y}\left[f\left(\tau ,y\left(y\left(t_0\right)\right)\right)\right]f\left(s,y\left(t_0\right)\right)\right)\right]f\left(v,y\left(t_0\right)\right)\right)dvdsd\tau[$]
[$]=\int _{t_0}^t\int _{t_0}^{\tau }\int _{t_0}^s\frac{1}{\sqrt{\tau }}\frac{1}{\sqrt{s}}\frac{y_0}{\sqrt{v}}dvdsd\tau =\frac{4}{3}t^{1.5} y_0[$]

And we get the first significant terms of the solution as
[$]y(t)[$][$]=y_0+2\sqrt{t}y_0+2t y_0+\frac{4}{3}t^{1.5}y_0+\text{...}[$]

You can verify that higher order terms obtained by continuing are indeed perfectly the same as in true solution. BTW this is a better example to show how time is integrated in various iterated integrals according to respective integration variables.
