Friends, I am writing this post to explain how easy Hermite polynomial regression is and how to use it in various diverse settings. Please recall that we have worked with hermite regression and also with joint distribution of random variables with hermite polynomials (which we did the last winters). 

I will try to give two different examples. In first example, we suppose that all explanatory regressor variables can be represented as a hermite series. In the second example, we will suppose that we have a few explanatory variables that can be represented by hermite polynomials and one (or more) explanatory variables that are represented by Legendre variables and we also have one dummy variable in regression.

The only important point is that hermite or in general orthogonal polynomial regression requires just one thing that all explanatory regressor random variables are converted into their respectively appropriate orthogonal polynomial form. It is not necessary to convert the  dependent regressand variable into any orthogonal polynomial. And rest of the regression just goes as a least squares regression.

So taking up the first example, let us suppose we have three explanatory variables [$]X_1[$],  [$]X_2[$] and [$]X_3[$] and we have a dependent variable whose value we want to find and is given as Y. And we are using first three hermite variables in the orthogonal polynomial regression

Suppose we have hermite expansions of [$]X_1[$],  [$]X_2[$] and [$]X_3[$] are given as

[$]X_1\, =\, a_0\, + \, a_1 \, H_1(Z_1) \,+ \, a_2 \, H_2(Z_1) \, + \, a_3 \, H_3(Z_1)[$]   Eq(1)
[$]X_2\, =\, b_0\, + \, b_1 \, H_1(Z_2) \,+ \, b_2 \, H_2(Z_2) \, + \, b_3 \, H_3(Z_2)[$]   Eq(2)
[$]X_3\, =\, c_0\, + \, c_1 \, H_1(Z_3) \,+ \, c_2 \, H_2(Z_3) \, + \, c_3 \, H_3(Z_3)[$]    Eq(3)

These hermite expansions would be found from the individual data points of (sample distribution of ) each explanatory variable  as we have repeatedly done in the past.

Suppose we have N data points of four dimensional joint data (3 regressors + 1 dependent variable) denoted by index n.

First we would have to invert the sample data of each explanatory variable [$]X_1[$],  [$]X_2[$] and [$]X_3[$] into respective  values of [$]Z_1[$],  [$]Z_2[$] and [$]Z_3[$]

So if [$]X_1[$] data is given as    [$]X_1(1),\, X_1(2),\, X_1(3),\, \ldots\, X_1(n), \, \ldots \, , \, X_1(N)[$], its inverse in terms of [$]Z_1[$] would be given as  
[$]Z_1(1),\, Z_1(2),\, Z_1(3),\, \ldots\, Z_1(n), \, \ldots \, , \, Z_1(N)[$], and this inverse would be found from Eq(1). 

Similalry from Eq(2) , we would be able to find  
[$]X_2(1),\, X_2(2),\, X_2(3),\, \ldots\, X_2(n), \, \ldots \, , \, X_2(N)[$], its inverse in terms of [$]Z_2[$] would be given as  
[$]Z_2(1),\, Z_2(2),\, Z_2(3),\, \ldots\, Z_2(n), \, \ldots \, , \, Z_2(N)[$],

and similarly 

from Eq(3), we would be able to find  
[$]X_3(1),\, X_3(2),\, X_3(3),\, \ldots\, X_3(n), \, \ldots \, , \, X_3(N)[$], its inverse in terms of [$]Z_3[$] would be given as  
[$]Z_3(1),\, Z_3(2),\, Z_3(3),\, \ldots\, Z_3(n), \, \ldots \, , \, Z_3(N)[$],

[$]U_1(1),\, U_1(2),\, U_1(3),\, \ldots\, U_1(n), \, \ldots \, , \, U_1(N)[$], and this inverse would be found from Eq(2). 

while [$]I(1), \, I(2), \,, \ldots, I(n)\, \ldots\,, \, I(N)[$] are the instances of dummy variable. 


Now we would have to set up the regression matrix as 


[$]\begin{bmatrix} 1 & H_1(Z_1(1)) & H_2(Z_1(1)) & H_3(Z_1(1)) & L_1(U_1(1)) & L_2(U_1(1)) & L_3(U_1(1)) & I(1) \\

\end{bmatrix}   \begin{bmatrix}c_0 \\ c_{11} \\ c_{12} \\ c_{13} \\ c_{21} \\ c_{22} \\ c_{3}\end{bmatrix} \\ \,=\, \begin{bmatrix} Y(1) \\ Y(2) \\ ... \\ ... \\  Y(n) \\ ... \\ ... \\ Y(N) \end{bmatrix}[$]

Again orthogonal polynomial regression goes just like a simple least square regression but all the explanatory variables have to be pre-processed and represented in form of the appropriate respective orthogonal polynomials and the rest just goes like a simple least squares regression.

In the graphs of last post, I took a time grid with ten seconds resolution. Crypto trades for 24 hours on binance so there are 8640 points on time grid. I recalculated hermite-series of the log-returns of each of the ten largest crypto-currencies with four coefficients (one zeroth hermite and three higher hermite polynomials) by ideal normal measure method (first sorting the data and then regressing on associated values of hermite polynomials) after every hundred points on our ten seconds grid. So calculation of hermite series was redone again and again after every 16.66 minutes. In each of the successive recalculations, all the data from start of the day was used so first regression had hundred data count while second regression had 200 data count and so on.  After re-calculation of hermite series of each crypto-currency, a regression was used to find the regression coefficients of bitcoin log returns on hermite polynomial representation of one period past log-returns of all the ten currencies including past returns of bitcoin itself. This regression was also repeated with the calculation of hermite series every 16.66 minutes or 100 time grid units of 10 seconds each. The latest regression coefficients available were used to predict the value of bitcoin log-returns as seen in graphs on previous post.

The choice of ten seconds grid, and choice of 100 intervals to redo all the hermite series and regression calculations was a bit arbitrary and friends might like to use their own judgement about increasing and decreasing these intervals. Since I had daily data in separate files and first regression was done after hundred time grid points, friends can see that in all the graphs red line for predicted returns starts after 100 time intervals since this was the time when first regression results became available in my set up.

The complete Crypto data was for 42 days and despite that I was recalculating regression after 16.66 minutes of data, it still took somewhere between 15 to 25 minutes to do all the calculation of 42 days of data on my simple laptop. 

My program made a hypothetical profit of 296,034 dollars for trading one bitcoin (which was roughly around 65,000 dollars) for 42 days(with 24 hours continuous trading) and made 127,969 trades making 2.31 dollars per trade. However unfortunately due to high commissions proportional to currency being traded on crypto currencies, this was not able to pay even the two side commissions on the Bitcoin trades for an ordinary user though it could have been hugely profitable for a lot of stock trading in most stock markets due to a different fixed (as opposed to proportional) commission structure in many stock markets.   

The seemingly high profits without commissions were possible since our hermite regression was able to capture quite a large share of total variance/variation in bitcoin price on ten seconds scale as could be seen through graphs in previous post. But in reality quite a few caveats would exist and making such profits would be impossible. But still I believe there is very good opportunity for people who could have very high trading volumes and they can make very handsome profits though actual profits would be substantially (possibly an order of magnitude smaller) smaller than in the simulation.

Friends, who want to make similar numerical experiments and analytics on their own.

The regression for estimation of coefficients is done in cross-correlation sense. I am not using hermite polynomials because purpose is to emphasize the time index only.

[$]\, Y(t) \, = \,c_0\, + \, c_1 \, X_1(t) \, + \, c_2 \, X_2(t) \, + \, c_3 \, X_3(t) \, + \, \ldots \, + \, c_N \, X_N(t) \,[$]

and prediction is done by using the equation ---- emphasizing time index only (In reality we would use hermite polynomials)

[$]\, Y(t+1) \, = \,c_0\, + \, c_1 \, X_1(t) \, + \, c_2 \, X_2(t) \, + \, c_3 \, X_3(t) \, + \, \ldots \, + \, c_N \, X_N(t) \,[$]

The regression is done in cross-correlation sense on the dependent assets at the same  time level as dependent asset on 10 seconds grid. Here in regression index of regressand and regressors both is t.
 
The Prediction is done in auto-correlation sense. The dependent variable at t+1 is being described by explanatory variables at time t. Regression coefficients are from previous step where regression was done in cross-correlation sense.

This works best on ten seconds grid.

Friends, I had been thinking about some interesting ideas regarding how to simultaneously predict a multi-dimensional variable from a set of explanatory variables. Least square regression and its variants tell us how to do something like this for a univariate dependent variable in regression. But what to do when there are more difficult joint multidimensional distribution based random variables that we want to predict.

And when I thought for sometime about it, I realized that we can actually do it with the infrastructure we have. We can see first scenario if different dimensions in multidimensional variable to be predicted are independent of each other, we can simply predict each dimension independently and combine the results.

But that is mostly not the case in real life. Let us suppose we want to predict a three dimensional variable and we have joint data of multidimensional variable to be predicted and also the joint data of explanatory variables. One thing we can do is to remove the effect of first dimension variable from second and third dimension variables by running a hermite regression or in some other intelligent way. And remove the effect of second variable from third variable again by running a regression or some other statistical method. And then run regressions on combined effect of first variable and combined effect of second variable and the remaining effect of third variable.

And then I recalled when we worked with solution of joint distribution of multivariate data with hermite polynomials of different factors (what we did this last winters), we described the joint multidimensional distribution of random variables in terms of orthogonal factors. We first calculated univariate hermite representation of first dimension in terms of hermite polynomials of [$]Z_1[$]. And then we removed the effect of [$]Z_1[$] from all other dimensions and described all this effect again in terms of hermite polynomials of [$]Z_1[$] and this was calculated separately for each variable from 2nd dimension to Nth dimension. So we can call [$]Z_1[$] an orthogonal factor (whose effect on various dimesnions is described by its own orthogonal hermite polynomials of various degree). We repeated this and described second dimension from which effect of [$]Z_1[$] had been removed by calculating a second factor [$]Z_2[$] whose hermite polynomials were able to explain all the remining effect of second dimension. And again removed the effect of [$]Z_2[$] from all other dimensions and described all this joint effect in terms of hermite polynomials of second factor [$]Z_2[$]. And similarly we divided the data into N different orthogonal factor Z's ranging from [$]Z_1[$] to [$]Z_N[$]. The first dimension of variable to be explained was described by factor [$]Z_1[$] and second dimension was described by [$]Z_1[$] and [$]Z_2[$] and last dimension among N dimensions of dependent variable was described by N Z's. So we basically converted data into N orthogonal factors(in a different sense than orthogonal polynomials) And when we want to find the effect of explanatory variables on multidimensional variables, we need to regress each of the orthogonal factor (of multidimensional dependent variable to be explained) represented by [$]Z_n[$]'s on the explanatory variables and then make a prediction of the multidimensional dependent variable by inverting back the regression result from factor coordinates  to the original random variable coordinates.

I think this way we can use mathematical analytics to solve for quite a few difficult problems where we use AI or ML to get half baked solutions with limited success.

\section{Correlations Between Different Hermite-Series Random Variables}
\label{sec4}


Since when we find a hermite series representation of a random variable, we decompose it in terms of orthogonal hermite polynomials. And variance of hermite series polynomial is given separately in terms of variances of hermite polynomials of different order.  Due to orthogonality property, correlations and covariances between two random variables only exist between hermite polynomials of both random variables having the same order. Due to this orthogonality property, When calculating conditional expectation of a random variable with respect to other random variables, we can separately calculate the conditional expectation of each orthogonal hermite polynomial of target random variable with respect to hermite polynomials of the explanatory random variables of the same order. 

Write calculation of covariance between two Hermite Series random variables.

\subsection{Estimation of Correlations Between two Hermite-Series Random variables }
\label{sec4.1}

 we write the equation for the correlation between first Hermite polynomial of Hermite-Series random variables X and Y as

\[\rho_{XY,1} \, = \,  \frac{\displaystyle\sum_{p=1}^{P} \, Z_{X,p} \, \, Z_{Y,p} \,}{\sqrt{\displaystyle\sum_{p=1}^{P} \, {Z_{X,p}}^2 \,\displaystyle\sum_{p=1}^{P} \, \, {Z_{Y,p}}^2 \,}}\]

Again, Please notice that for calculation of above equation, we first have to find the specific values of \(Z_{X,p}\) and  \(Z_{Y,p}\) that are associated with particular pth observation of  \(X_{p}\) and  \(Y_{p}\). This has to be done by numerical inversion method we described in section ().
For Finding Correlation between second hermite polynomials, we write the equation for the correlation between second hermite polynomial of X and Y as

\[\rho_{XY,2} \, = \,  \frac{\displaystyle\sum_{p=1}^{P} \, ({Z_{X,p}}^2-1) \, \, ({Z_{Y,p}}^2-1) \,}{\sqrt{\displaystyle\sum_{p=1}^{P} \, {(Z_{X,p}^2-1)}^2 \,\displaystyle\sum_{p=1}^{P} \, \, {({Z_{Y,p}}^2-1})^2 \,}}\]

similarly for nth hermite polynaomial correlations, we would write as

\[\rho_{XY,n} \, = \,  \frac{\displaystyle\sum_{p=1}^{P}\, H_n(Z_{X,p}) \, \, H_n(Z_{Y,p}) \,}{\sqrt{\displaystyle\sum_{p=1}^{P} \, {H_n(Z_{X,p})}^2 \,\displaystyle\sum_{p=1}^{P} \, \, {H_n(Z_{Y,p})}^2 \,}}\]

Please note that correlations are in joint products of values of hermite polynomials. The coefficients of Hermite Polynomials in respective Hermite-Series are only required for covariances between hermite polynomials.

\subsection{Conditional Expectation of a Hermite-series Random variable given the Value of Another Correlated Hermite Series Random variable }
\label{sec4.2}

Conditional expectation was earlier known mostly for normal random variable with just a few exceptions. Normal random variable is just one and most basic hermite polynomial but covariances/correlations and conditional expectations between higher hermite polynomials go just like between instances of first hermite polynomial which is the normal random variable itself. By converting a non-random variables into hermite-series, we can easily find conditional expectations between non-normal random variables which was earlier not possible.
As we said at the start of the section that orthogonality property of hermite polynomials and decomposition of variance into orthogonal parts means that variances and correlations between random variables exist between hermite polynomials of the same order. And this implies that conditional expectation of a random variable with respect to other random variables can be divided into separate covariance/correlation based relationships between hermite polynomials of different random variables of the same order. Further implying that the conditional expectation of a target random variable based on other explanatory random variables can be described by sum of separate conditional expectations of each hermite polynomial of target random variable with respect to hermite polynomials of the explanatory random variables of the same order. 

First we describe univariate conditional expectation of one Hermite-Series random variable on another Hermite-Series random variable and also calculate variance of this conditional expectation.   

Suppose we know univariate hermite series of target variable X and explanatory variable Y. We define some basic relationships again for clarity


\[X(Z_X)\,=\, \sum\limits_{n=0}^{N}\, b_n\, H_n(Z_X) \,=\, \sum\limits_{n=0}^{N}\, X_n \]
\[Y(Z_Y)\,=\, \sum\limits_{n=0}^{N}\, c_n\, H_n(Z_Y) \,=\, \sum\limits_{n=0}^{N}\, Y_n \]

Normal random variable is just first hermite polynomial and covariances/correlations and conditional expectations of higher hermite polynomials go just analogously to those of first hermite polynomial.
The conditional expectation of nth hermite polynomial based term (with coefficient \(c_n\)) of the target variable Y is given in terms of covariance \(Cov_{XY,n}\)between nth polynomials of both random variables X and Y and variance \(Var_{X,n}\) of the nth hermite polynomial of explanatory variable X as 
\[E[Y_n\,|\, X_n]\,= \, E[c_n \, H_n(Z_Y)\,|\,H_n(Z_X)]\,=\,\frac{Cov_{XY,n}}{Var_{X,n}}\,b_n \, H_n(Z_X) \]
\[Var[Y_n\,|\, X_n]\,= Var[c_n \, H_n(Z_Y)\,|\,H_n(Z_X)]\,=\,Var_{Y,n}\,-\frac{{Cov_{XY,n}}^2}{Var_{X,n}}\,=(1\,-\, {\rho_{XY,n}}^2) \, Var_{Y,n}\, \]
We can write sum of conditional expectations of all hermite polynomials of target variable Y with respect to explanatory variable as
\[ \sum\limits_{n=1}^{N} E[c_n \, H_n(Z_Y),|\,H_n(Z_X)]\,=\,\sum\limits_{n=1}^{N}\frac{Cov_{XY,n}}{Var_{X,n}}\,b_n \, H_n(Z_X) \]
But we know that conditional expectation of Y is equal to sum of conditional expectations of each orthogonal polynomial of Y including the zeroth hermite polynomial which is the unconditional mean of Y itself, so we can write
\[E[Y \, | \, X]=\mu_Y+ \sum\limits_{n=1}^{N} E[c_n \, H_n(Z_Y)\, | \,H_n(Z_X) ]\]
which means that 
\[E[Y\, | \, X]=\mu_Y+\,\sum\limits_{n=1}^{N}\frac{Cov_{XY,n}}{Var_{X,n}}\,b_n \, H_n(Z_X) \]
and 
\[Var[Y\, | \, X]=\,\sum\limits_{n=1}^{N}\,Var_{Y,n}\,-\frac{{Cov_{XY,n}}^2}{Var_{X,n}}\, \]
\[=\,\sum\limits_{n=1}^{N}\,(1\,-\, {\rho_{XY,n}}^2) \, Var_{Y,n}\,\, \]
We have given the formulas for conditional expected value and the variance of a non-Gaussian random variable Y given another non-Gausain random variable X, And now non-Gaussian Hermite-series of Y conditional on X could be given as
\[ Y\, | \, X=\mu_Y+\,\sum\limits_{n=1}^{N}\frac{Cov_{XY,n}}{Var_{X,n}}\,b_n \, H_n(Z_X)+\,\sum\limits_{n=1}^{N} \sqrt{(1\,-\, {\rho_{XY,n}}^2)} \, c_n \, H_n(Z_Y)\]
In the above equation, the first summation term in the RHS sums to a point value which is conditional expected mean and second summation in the RHS is the unexplained non-gaussian variance structure of the distribution in terms of hermite polynomials which it inherits from the original distribution.

\subsection{De-correlating the effect of a Hermite-series Random variable from Another Correlated Hermite Series Random variable }
\label{sec4.3}

In order to remove the effect of a random variable X from another correlated random variable Y, we remove the conditional expected value of \(E\left[Y|X\right]\) from the observed value of Y. This decorrelated value can be written as an equation as
\[ \tilde{\tilde{X}} \,=\, X\, -\, E\left[Y|X \right] \, \]
This removal of effect of some correlated random variable could have some very interesting applications in finance where we want to find the effect of a particular variable after removing effects of other variables.


\subsection{Calculating the true distribution structure in Uni-variate Conditional Expectation of a Correlated Random variable }
\label{sec4.4}

In order to find the true distribution associated with univariate conditional expectation of a random variable, we perform the de-correlation operation discussed in previous subsection on jointly observed values of data sample of X and Y, we can find that \(\tilde{\tilde{Y}}\) represents the true residual part of Y that has not been explained by the variable X. Suppose we have a sample of P values represented by \(\tilde{\tilde{Y}}_p\, , p\,=\,1,...,P\), we use this sample of values of \(\tilde{\tilde{Y}}_p\, \) to construct an associated Hermite Series from one of the methods discussed in section 3.00000.
Suppose this Hermite Series to be found from data of \(\tilde{\tilde{Y}}_p\, \) is given as

\[\tilde{\tilde{Y}}(Z_Y)\,=\, \sum\limits_{n=0}^{N}\, \tilde{\tilde{c}}_n\, H_n(Z_Y) \]   Eq(A0)

We can write the variance of conditional expectation of X as
\[Var \left[ Y|X \right] \,=\,Var \left[ \tilde{\tilde{Y}}_n \right] \,\,=\, \,\sum\limits_{n=1}^{N}\,{\tilde{\tilde{c_n}}}^2 n! \]
The above point variance should perfectly match the conditional point variance we found in section 4.2 also given here as
\[Var[Y\, | \, X]=\,\sum\limits_{n=1}^{N}\,(1\,-\, {\rho_{XY,n}}^2) \, Var_{Y,n}\,\,=\, \,\sum\limits_{n=1}^{N}\,(1\,-\, {\rho_{XY,n}}^2) \,{c_n}^2 n! \]
However we stress again that even though point variance is perfectly matched, the true structure of variance in distribution sense is not given by  Eq(A) ( though usually close enough but not always)
\[ \,\sum\limits_{n=1}^{N} \sqrt{(1\,-\, {\rho_{XY,n}}^2)} \, c_n \, H_n(Z_Y)\]
and is rather given by Eq(B)
\[\tilde{\tilde{Y}}(Z_Y)\,=\, \sum\limits_{n=0}^{N}\, \tilde{\tilde{c}}_n\, H_n(Z_Y) \]
We want to mention again that point value of second central moment, variance, would be the same true value in both Eq(A) and Eq(B) but Eq(B) gives the true distribution of unexplained part of random variable Y. The reason is that once effect of conditional expected value is removed, even though total residual variance can be precisely calculated but there is re-arrangement or re-distribution of variance among hermite polynomials and their coefficients cannot be analytically calculated through correlations (which is reasonably close but not true) and have to be found from the data as we have done in Eq(A0).

So now the conditional distribution of Y|X would be written as
\[ Y\, | \, X=\, E \left[ Y|X \right] + \, \tilde{\tilde{Y}}(Z_Y)\,\]
\[ Y\, | \, X=\mu_Y+\,\sum\limits_{n=1}^{N}\frac{Cov_{XY,n}}{Var_{X,n}}\,b_n \, H_n(Z_X)+\, \sum\limits_{n=0}^{N}\, \tilde{\tilde{c}}_n\, H_n(Z_Y)\]
\[ =\mu_Y+\,\sum\limits_{n=1}^{N}\,\rho_{XY,n} \, c_n \, H_n(Z_X)+\, \sum\limits_{n=0}^{N}\, \tilde{\tilde{c}}_n\, H_n(Z_Y)\]

We want to emphasize that same data that is used to estimate correlation based variance and distribution in subsection(4.2) can be used for calculation of true conditional distribution as described in this section (4.4) and once above equation has been calculated from data, we can use it for conditional distribution of random variable Y as single (or more) values of X become available just as easily as we did for correlation based conditional distribution.
