But quickly some ideas about different types of mean reversion.

1. Standard mean reversion.

When auto-correlations and cross-correlations are small and there is little emotion in the market. Our emotion indicators are close to zero. Market continues to move between bid ask with only small changes in mid-price. This market state/regime might not be very attractive for medium frequency trading but when this standard mean reverting state switches to another possibly trending state, we might want to start/end a trade there.

2. Mean-reversion towards broader market.

When broader gets an emotion and there is a sizable increase/decrease in other market stocks, and our stock in question might not have moved enough in direction of the market, we might want to start a trade in the hope that traded stock would also follow the market in the direction of increase/decrease in the market.

3. Corrective mean reversion.

This type of mean reversion takes place in high volatility stocks and it is very desirable to trade in them when doing medium frequency trading.
This is mean reversion that takes place when there is a short term correction in the stock(on a minute/smaller scale or so) after the stock increases/decreases quite a bit due to high volatility (something that is not sustainable or statistically too large) and we make a trade after a relatively large increase/decrease in the stock price hoping that price would decrease/increase after a large increase/decrease in price. This type of mean reversion should not be confused with standard mean reversion and corrective mean reversion might not have negative auto-correlation. And I still have to think more how the patterns of autocorrelation and change of autocorrelation associated with this type of mean reversion.

We have a universe of M stocks and The price of mth stock at time t is given as [$]X_m(t)[$]. We convert these stock values into log returns so they are easy to use in our set up.

Friends, I have been thinking about whether stock and market state indicators could be further improved. And in order to do that we have to look closely at stock and market state indicators I wrote in the previous post.

We can make an indicator for market across the M stocks over four 15 second periods as

[$]IM_{state}(t)\, =\, \sum\limits_{m=1}^{M} \,\, w_{m} \, \Large{ \frac{\sum\limits_{k=0}^{3} \, R_m(t-k)\,}{\sqrt{\sum\limits_{k=0}^{3} \, {R_m(t-k)}^2\,}}}[$] 

The above indicator will describe the current state of the market (that is reasonably fleeting and will change in a minute or two). 
I kept the weights [$]\, w_{m} \, =\, \frac{1}{M}[$]

 corresponding indicator for a single stock would be 

[$]IS_{state}(t)\, =\, \Large{ \frac{\sum\limits_{k=0}^{3} \, R(t-k)\,}{\sqrt{\sum\limits_{k=0}^{3} \, {R(t-k)}^2\,}}}\,[$] 

 
First I want to outline the logic of these indicators. Squared sum of log returns in the denominator of stock and market state indicators is serving two purposes. 

First Purpose: it makes sure if variance in the component returns (in sample used to calculate the indicator) is low, it gives them a larger weight and if variance between components returns in sample used to calculate the indicator is high, it gives them a smaller weight. But as a second thought, this could be better done by variance rather than using second moment.

Second Purpose: When we are doing calculations of market state indicators, there different stocks some of which have larger returns (in absolute sense: negative or positive) on average while some other stocks have smaller returns in absolute sense. But when we are adding them across the market, we want to give them similar weights (if other considerations are not involved) and would want to use the same currency for all stocks irrespective of larger or smaller absolute returns while adding sum of their returns to make a market state indicator. So when second moment of returns of a stock in the indicator is larger (returns are larger in absolute sense), its weight decreases in the market state indicator and if second moment of some other stock is smaller (returns are smaller on average), its weight is relatively higher when added to make a market state indicator. So division by second moments serves a good purpose of making sure that stocks with relatively larger returns and relatively smaller returns get a similar weight(if other considerations are not involved) when adding them to make a market indicator. 

So we notice that we would be better off if for first purpose, we replace second moments by variance. And we need to keep second moment in our calculations when we assign weights to different state indicators and add them to make a market indicator.

And I found out that this new idea does largely improves the performance of the model when we alter our stock and market state indicators according to these new considerations.

So our new improved indicator for market state now becomes 


[$]IM_{state}(t)\, =\, \sum\limits_{m=1}^{M} \,\, w_{m} \, \Large{ \frac{\sum\limits_{k=0}^{Lag-1} \, R_m(t-k)\,}{\sqrt{\sum\limits_{k=0}^{Lag-1} \,{\Big[ {R_m(t-k)}\,-\,\sum\limits_{k=0}^{Lag-1} \, R_m(t-k)\,/Lag \,\Big]}^2}}}[$] 


The above indicator has sum of returns of each stock in numerator and square root of their variance in denominator. But we would also have to alter the weights of the stocks and make them inversely proportional to second moment of each stock in the sample. This is done by defining  [$]w_m[$]  as


[$]\, w_m \, =\, \LARGE{ \frac{ \frac{\,1\,}{\sqrt{\sum\limits_{k=0}^{Lag-1} \, {R_m(t-k)}^2\,}}}{ \sum\limits_{m=1}^{M} \,\, \frac{\,1\,}{\sqrt{\sum\limits_{k=0}^{Lag-1} \, {R_m(t-k)}^2\,}}}}[$] 


And our new improved indicators of state of a single stock now becomes


[$]IS_{state}(t)\, =\, \Large{ \frac{\sum\limits_{k=0}^{Lag-1} \, R(t-k)\,}{\sqrt{\sum\limits_{k=0}^{Lag-1} \,{\Big[ {R(t-k)}\,-\,\sum\limits_{k=0}^{Lag-1} \, R(t-k)\,/Lag \,\Big]}^2}}}[$]
