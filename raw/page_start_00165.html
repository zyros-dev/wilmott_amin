@Paul, as a start, I was trying to avoid singular initial value problems. But I will definitely come to this in a few days.

@amike, You are very right that most of the solutions are identical to power series solutions or many times to taylor series solutions. In fact I believe that there is a Taylor series analogue of every solution I find out  using my method. If you look at the ODE books, the problems that are solved usually by Taylor series and power series are very limited to a very small subset of ODEs. As I said there is a Taylor series analogue of every solution I find using my method which hints towards universal applicability of taylor series like methods. Most examples I gave in paper were very basic and the solution was mostly known so that reader could become familiar with the method using very familiar examples. However you could see how easily my method solved the example for second order nonlinear ODE given in post 150. Example 5 on page 16 of my paper. People never traditionally used taylor series or power series methods on these odes. However if you do a Taylor series expansion on that ODE, you will find the same solution as I gave. The crux of the method is given in page 1-5 of the paper. It helps us determine how to take the nested derivatives and partials in a simple sequential way. Again there is a taylor series analogue of every expansion I have given in my paper and that hints at the universal applicability of the Taylor series and related methods. My method has the advantage that I have done the same things in the form of integrals and when time/independent variable is present in the differential equation ,we get a better approximation since we have integrated the dependence of independent variable present in the differential equation like a one dimensional integration.
Again answering amike's observation that each expansion using my methods is going to power series, or taylor series solution of the problem, I would say the solution using my method goes to analytic solution as we increase the terms and therefore it has to agree with the formal power series solutions wherever they can be found since they are expansion of the same analytic solution. For the same analytic solution, if we have expanded it purely in powers x^n, it would definitely make sense to all of us that  each power x^n should have the same coefficients whatever method we use for evaluation of that function unless there are some complex(in sense of complexity and not complex varaibles) functions in coefficients that have their own expansions.  
As amike suggested, I am doing a revision of my paper adding many other problems that are not basic and hint at near universal applicability of the method. I thank amike for his critical review.

Cuch, thanks for adding to discussion. My approach is different from power series approach but agrees with power series approach whenever power series approach is valid.

@Cuch, my approach is more close to taylor series approach as opposed to power series. If we consider a first order ODE, in taylor series approach we take  higher partials w.r.t independent variable and higher partials w.r.t dependent variable and connect them to independent variable through chain rule. But once we have analytically integrated the differential equation w.r.t independent variable on the first level, we do not need further partial derivatives w.r.t independent variable and we only need to take higher partials w.r.t dependent variables and use chain rule and integrate each term with respect to dependent variable and this is where we diverge from classical taylor series method.  
In our solution to  y'=f(t,y), we do not need higher partials with respect to t which would be needed in taylor series method. Again since we analytically integrated t in method of iterated integrals, we do not need these higher partials with respect to independent variable. Of course, this is assuming that we can analytically integrate t in y'=f(t,y).
