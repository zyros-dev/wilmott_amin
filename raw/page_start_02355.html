Friends, I went ahead to try a sort of simple and new parametric model based on mean reversion. This model is suitable for most of the days but it is not suitable for days when market is trending. For most of the models to work well, we need to be able to tell when market is mean reverting and when it is trending. And use mean reverting models when the market regime is mean reverting (which is mostly the case). Last year, we tried to know when market is trending and when it is mean reverting from moments of the Z's associated with market returns. Mean reverting processes have little deep tails while trending processes have larger higher moments. But this is a different (though very related) topic of research.

Again, I tried this new  parametric mean reversion model for stocks. It gave me surprisingly great results. This is like a lower side of high frequency model with a very large number of trades. The model made 11251 trades in 40 days (first training day is not used in back-testing). It made a profit of 908 dollars based on trade of one share. This share could be any of the top ten stocks of Nasdaq in the same May and June data  of year 2021. When conditions are right multiple trades in different stocks could be open but no more than one share of any stock is traded at any time. It earned a profit of 8.07 cents per trade which is extremely good for a high frequency-like model. I applied the model on all days and if I had made a good way to find out when market is trending and not have used the parametric mean reverting model on those days, the results would have been extremely better. Though results were still good, I would recommend anyone who uses this model to first also find out a criteria when market is mean reverting and when market is trending and use the model only when market is mean reverting. Again, we can possibly find out this regime from use of Z-series moments.

The logic of the model is that greater the sum of deviations of the stock returns from zero, more likely are the new returns going to move against this accumulated deviation. 

First I want to rewrite some indicators and other preliminaries from the past.

Friends, let us suppose we have financial data of equities on a 15 sec grid (My data is for 41 working days from May and June of 2021). We have a universe of M stocks and The price of mth stock at time t is given as [$]X_m(t)[$]. We convert these stock values into log returns so they are easy to use in our set up.

[$]R_m(t)\,=\, \log(X_m(t))\,-\, \log(X_m(t-1))[$]

You can use your own definition of returns and possibly label above as  [$]R_m(t-1)\,[$] or divide by time increment etc. 

From the moments of above returns, we make a Z-series/Hermite-series representation of returns of each asset. I made a hermite series of returns from previous day and used it for next day (but that was because I was a bit lazy) and you can add information to Z-series from running day at several discrete points in time. This Z-series expansion of returns is given as

[$]R_m(t)\,=\, a_0\, +\, a_1 \, Z_m(t)\,+\, a_2 \, {Z_m(t)}^2\,+\, a_3 \, {Z_m(t)}^3\,+\, \ldots[$]

During the market time on 15 seconds grid, whenever we notice a new return [$]R_m(t)[$] in the market, we immediately know the associated [$]Z_m(t)[$] in the market. 

For positive returns, the [$]Z_m(t)[$] would likely be positive and for negative returns [$]Z_m(t)[$] would likely be positive. Similarly for very large positive returns [$]Z_m(t)[$] would likely be very large positive and vice versa. 

A simple Stock return and associated [$]Z_m[$] continues to fluctuate between positive and negative states and these states last from half a minute to 2-3 minutes. Our indicator for state of a single stock would be 

[$]IS_{state}\, =\, \sum\limits_{k=0}^{3} \, \, w_k\, Z_m(t-k)\,[$] 

If you want to know about Stock regime in general as opposed to fleeting state of the Stock, you could do the above indicator for a longer lag as

[$]IS_{regime}\, =\, \sum\limits_{k=0}^{60}  \, w_{k}\, Z_m(t-k)\,[$] 

Now our parametric model for the prediction of next stock return [$]\bar{Z_m}(t+1)[$] is 

[$] \bar{Z_m}(t+1) \, =\, Z_m(t) \, - \, c_1 \, Z_m(t)\, -\, c_2\,  IS_{state} \, -\, c_3 \, IS_{regime}\, [$]

with       [$]c_1\, =\, .75[$],     [$]c_2\, =\, .6[$],      [$]c_3\, =\, .45[$],

[$]
    I(t)= 
\begin{cases}
    1 ,& \text{if } \bar{Z_m}(t+1)\geq .65\\
    -1 ,& \text{if } \bar{Z_m}(t+1)\leq -.525\\
    0,              & \text{otherwise}
\end{cases}
[$]

All the numerical values of constants and thresholds I gave in above few equations where found by me by playing iteratively with the model so as to maximize the profit. Obviously these values would be different for every time series but I have given my values for reference only.

At the start of every 15 second interval, we calculate the rule [$]I(t)[$] from above. If [$]I(t)[$] is equal to one, it means that next returns are predicted to be positive. If If [$]I(t)[$] is equal to negative one, it means that next returns are predicted to be negative. And if If [$]I(t)[$] is equal to zero, it means that we are undecisive about next returns. 

So whenever [$]I(t)[$] is positive one, we close any previous sell trade if it is open and open new buy trade.  And when [$]I(t)[$] is negative one, we close any buy trade if it is already open and open new sell trade.

 The model made 11251 trades in 40 days (first training day is not used in back-testing). It made a profit of 908 dollars based on trade of one share. This share could be any of the top ten stocks of Nasdaq in the same May and June data  of year 2021. It earned a profit of 8.07 cents per trade which is extremely good for a high frequency-like model. Sum of Both sides commissions is less than one cent.

If I had applied the model only on days when stocks are mean reverting, the results would have been very impressive with almost no losses. 

Here is the graph that shows profit/Loss of the model trading strategy on each of the forty trading days.




Friends, it just seems to me that Z-series and related parameters are really the right tools for many of the models and strategies when it comes to stocks trading. I have previously played with the market for years with very little luck with just one exception. But I have been able to build reasonably decent models in just past few days. I think it is only because I used Z-series based statistics that are totally standard and take away a lot of complexity associated with stocks when we use these statistics intelligently.

Friends, in the context of last two posts, I want to mention again that we covered this in probably May or June last year in Summers when a time series is mean reverting and when it is trending. We have to apply that criteria in real time to find out when our time series is mean reverting and when it is trending so we know when to use the above model strategy from previous post. 
As I recall, eighth moment of time series when it is trending is far higher than eighth moment of a time series when it is mean reverting. We also would like to look at skew moments. I think I posted a matlab program regarding that. So that kind of criteria can be very helpful to know when to apply the above model in real time and when to avoid using it.
Thinking again, I think, in order to find mean reversion, we can use another strategy. If we are on a fifteen seconds grid, We can calculate higher moments (possibly eighth moment) on a minute level returns granularity. Due to cancellations, mean reverting series would have smaller higher moments and due to relative lack of cancellations, trending time series would have higher moments. If we compare higher moments, on lowest possible granularity of 15 seconds, there might not be any remarkable difference in higher moments since eighth moment would not be able to differentiate between positive return followed by positive return (trending) and positive return followed by negative return (mean reverting). However when aggregated and moments are calculated on aggregated returns, the difference in higher moments should start to become obvious due to cancellations (due to mean reversion) when aggregated.

Friends, I think I have some more clear idea about calculation of local dynamic correlations so I thought I should write them in this post.

Let us suppose, we have a time series given as

[$]X_1,\, X_2 ,\, X_3, \, \ldots,\, X_{t-2}, \, X_{t-1}, \, X_t[$] 

We make another two series out of the above series. One series would consist of sum of two adjacent (in time) realizations of the above variable and second series would consist of sum of squares of two adjacent (in time) realizations of the above variable.

We write as



[$]V_1(t)\, =\, X_{t-1} \, +\, X_{t} \,[$] 

while 

[$]V_2(t)\, =\, {X_{t-1}}^2 \, +\, {X_{t}}^2 \,[$]

We come up with an indicator that is directly proportional to correlation

[$]\, I_{Corr}(t)\, =\Large{\, \frac{E[{V_1}^2]}{E[V_2]}}[$] 

[$]\LARGE{=\, \frac{\sum\limits_{k=0}^{Lag-1} \, {V_1(t-k)}^2}{\sum\limits_{k=0}^{Lag-1} \, V_2(t-k)}}[$]

[$]I_{Corr}(t)[$] remains strictly between zero and two. At value of zero, it means correlation is equal to minus one. At  [$]I_{Corr}(t)=1[$], it means, correlation is equal to zero. At  [$]I_{Corr}(t)=2[$], it means, correlation is equal to positive one.
In general , I suppose that local correlation at a time t is given as

[$] Corr(t)\,=\,\rho(t)\,=\,  I_{Corr}(t)\,-\, 1[$]

I used the above method to calculate auto-correlations in intraday financial data. As we know auto-correlations are notorious to be very unstable in financial data and it shows up in the graph. Even though we can see a pattern. The indicator calculated strictly remains between zero and two. I calculated graph below with a Lag=5. You can see in second graph that there is enough clustering of correlation.

Friends, I hope that we would be doing interesting things with correlations. One interesting thing is how the dynamics of auto-correlations and cross-correlations are related to each other. We know that cross correlations are generally more stable. But in evolution of a multivariate time series, cross-correlations are not independent of auto-correlations. 
I will try to write a more detailed post with equations later but here are some insights that I thought about. We can divide increments of a time series on every time step into an auto-correlated part and another orthogonal part. As it seems, in order to maintain relatively more stable cross-correlations, that auto-correlated parts of each (of the multivariate) time series have to be correlated across different time series but I believe that orthogonal part (even though orthogonal in time) also has cross-correlations  with orthogonal parts of other time series (in order to maintain relatively stable cross-sectional correlations across different time series.)
I think we need a small but comprehensive set of equations that describes how auto-correlations and cross-correlations and orthogonal parts are related to each other.

Friends, I want to try to explain the auto-correlation formula in a bit more detail here.

Copying the preliminaries from previous post, we have

Let us suppose, we have a time series given as

[$]X_1,\, X_2 ,\, X_3, \, \ldots,\, X_{t-2}, \, X_{t-1}, \, X_t[$] 

We make another two series out of the above series. One series would consist of sum of two adjacent (in time) realizations of the above variable and second series would consist of sum of squares of two adjacent (in time) realizations of the above variable.

We write as



[$]V_1(t)\, =\, X_{t-1} \, +\, X_{t} \,[$] 

while 

[$]V_2(t)\, =\, {X_{t-1}}^2 \, +\, {X_{t}}^2 \,[$]

We come up with an indicator that is directly proportional to correlation

[$]\, I_{Corr}(t)\, =\Large{\, \frac{E[{V_1}^2]}{E[V_2]}}[$] 

[$]\LARGE{=\, \frac{\sum\limits_{k=0}^{Lag-1} \, {V_1(t-k)}^2}{\sum\limits_{k=0}^{Lag-1} \, V_2(t-k)}}[$]

Now consider the above sums in more detail

[$]\sum\limits_{k=0}^{Lag-1} \, {V_1(t-k)}^2[$]
[$]=\sum\limits_{k=0}^{Lag-1} \, {\big[\, X_{t-1} \, +\, X_{t} \, \big]}^2[$]
[$]=\sum\limits_{k=0}^{Lag-1} \, {\big[\, {X_{t-1}}^2 \, +\, {X_{t}}^2 \,+ \, 2  \, \, X_{t-1} \, X_{t} \big]}[$]

We identify that  [$]\sum\limits_{k=0}^{Lag-1} \, \big[\, {X_{t-1}}^2 \, +\, {X_{t}}^2 \, \big] \,=\, 2\, Var[X] \, [$]
while  [$]\sum\limits_{k=0}^{Lag-1} \,  \, 2  \, \, X_{t-1} \, X_{t} \, =\, 2\,COV[X_t,X_{t-1}][$]

I write down the auto-correlation formula again as

[$]\LARGE{\, \frac{\sum\limits_{k=0}^{Lag-1} \, {V_1(t-k)}^2}{\sum\limits_{k=0}^{Lag-1} \, V_2(t-k)}}[$]

[$]\LARGE{\,= \frac{\,\sum\limits_{k=0}^{Lag-1} \, \big[ \,{X_{t-1}}^2 \, +\, {X_{t}}^2 \,+ \, 2  \,  \, X_{t-1} \, X_{t} \big]}{\sum\limits_{k=0}^{Lag-1} \,\big[\, {X_{t-1}}^2 \, +\, {X_{t}}^2 \, \big]}}[$]

[$]\LARGE{\,= \frac{\,\sum\limits_{k=0}^{Lag-1} \, \big[ \,{X_{t-1}}^2 \, +\, {X_{t}}^2 \, \big]+ \, 2  \, \,\sum\limits_{k=0}^{Lag-1} \, \big[ \, \, X_{t-1} \, X_{t} \big]}{\sum\limits_{k=0}^{Lag-1} \,\big[\, {X_{t-1}}^2 \, +\, {X_{t}}^2 \, \big]}}[$]

[$]\LARGE{\,= \frac{ \, 2 \, Var[X_t] \, + \, 2 \, COV[X_t,X_{t-1}]}{ \, 2 \, Var[X_t] \, }}[$]

[$]=\,  1 \, + \LARGE{\, \frac{ \, 2 \, COV[X_t,X_{t-1}]}{ \, 2 \, Var[X_t] \, }}[$]

[$]= \, 1 \, + \, \rho(t) [$]

I have not applied any means in the formula since in financial data the mean of log-returns is very close to zero. If needed, you can do your improvise upon the formula to subtract means where appropriate.
The formula would however apply only to auto-correlations but that is where we need it the most. And auto-correlations was our subject of discussion in the context of trading. 
 Even for very small lags, the auto-correlation remains strictly between minus one and one.

I have deleted and edited a previous post to make this new post to make it more clear to understand.

Friends, some basic ideas about preliminary analytics that we need to do from information of previous days before we start market trading in current day. 

When I mention Z-series below, it means Z-series derived from past data of log-returns of stocks or other financial instruments over an equi-spaced grid in time.

First point is that in market everything is relative (usually relative to average market conditions in the sense that opportunity/Loss depends upon how much the market has deviated from average market conditions in positive or adverse ways). We can use Z-series and derived indicators in mathematical trading since they can provide us precious information in a very standardized and understandable way. I was previously using market data from past one day to calculate Z-series that would be used today. This Z-series was recalculated everyday and therefore all reference indicators and decision making was not relative to a single standard which (even though many times partially helpful in trading) was actually problematic. But I believe that purpose of Z-series is to give us a standard reference for comparison of today's market conditions with respect to average previous market conditions in a standard way. Therefore, we would also like to run prospective strategies on previous 7/15/or more  days of data (using a single Z-series derived from past 7/15/or more days of data)  as if we were back-testing and try to know what strategies were successful in training data of past few weeks and also know the range of parameters when they were successful. And later in live trading on the current day, as I said that market is a concept in being relative, we would trade different strategies(using the same single reference Z-series that we had derived from 7/15 or more days of past data) only when the associated parameters are equal to or more extreme (in the strategy-positive direction) than the successful range of parameters(associated with each successful strategy in historical backtesting of past week) as found from back-testing in past seven/fifteen days. This way a single reference Z-series calculated from past few weeks serves as a single standard reference for all indicators and decision making on historical data and the same indicators and similar decision making could be carried forward to the live trading on current day.

However some information is so basic that we need to fix it in our Z-series if that happens. For example mean of Z-series has to be kept strictly at zero even if the mean from past data is different from zero. This mean would have to be kept zero even in back-testing over past seven days of historical data. This is because a positive or negative mean of Z-series from before can turn our market direction in reverse direction or increase or decrease the intensity of our indicators. This is because if previous mean of Z-series in positive, our calculated Z increment(from inversion of returns) will be positive only if the mean of today's data is large than the previous mean. In short, for directional indicators based on Z-series we want a standardized mean of zero and we would have to change the mean of every reference Z-series, used for decision making in both historical backtesting and live trading, to zero.
