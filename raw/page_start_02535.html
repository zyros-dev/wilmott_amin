Friends, I had a few thoughts that I wanted to share for those people who joined the thread later and have not been following it for years.

It is important to distinguish between a random variable X and its density p(X). In a Z-series, we represent random variable X by a series in another random variable which is standard normal Z. We do not get density of X directly from Z-series. When dealing with continuous random variables, a large number of them can be represented in terms of derivatives of each other by another series rescaling the coefficients on these derivatives.
For example, if for example if you graph a standard normal as a function of X using inverse function derivatives from Z-series of X, it will be a curve and not a density.

For calculation of densities, you have to use the formula

\[p_X(X)\,=\,p_Z(Z)\,\frac{dZ}{dX} \]  Eq(1)

This can be easily calculated on a grid since X and Z and their derivative corresponding to each other are known on a grid.

If you want to analytically find the density of X as an analytic expression in X, you would have to  change variables in standard normal density formula as

\[p_X(X)\,=\,p_Z(Z(X))\,\frac{dZ(X)}{dX} \]      Eq(2)

and densities of functions of X would have to be found from change of variable in density of X possibly found from above expression.

When we invert coefficients from hermite series regression of X on target variable Y and change variables in regression result from standard normal to another series expression in X, we do not find density of this function of X. After this change of variable in regression result, We merely find the relationship Y=f(X). We do  not get density of Y or density of f(X). If you are looking for analytic expression of density/function of Y=f(X) , you will have to do it by doing change of variable with respect to normal density as suggested in Eq(2).
