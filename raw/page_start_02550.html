Friends, I was able to convert the regression coefficients from hermite polynomials to original variables with R-Squared equal to or exceeding the R-Squared in the hermite polynomial variables.

But then I noticed something far more interesting. We could regress the hermite coefficients of SDE densities directly in parameters of the SDEs with exactly the same accurcay without having to map them on normal density and converting them into hermite polynomial series. All we need is to expand the SDE density hermite coefficients directly in terms of a sum of various power series in parameters and products of parameters directly around the median of each series variable (that is each variable of the power series is a parameter of the density or products of parameters). The only thing probably needed was that each of the variable in the component power series had to be expanded around its median in all the individual power series. This is even more remarkable and makes everything very simple. However there were potential problems with matrix inversions in original variables that were not there when we mapped the regression variables on normal density and then regressed.

Here is a graph from mean of the SDE density directly from a regression on original variables with the same sample we used earlier. It had an R-Squared of .999997. Here green (true) and red (our density from original variable regression) lines were overlaid and you cannot see any red at all because of high precision.( I inverted the order or variables in graph and then I could not see any green at all).

Here is a graph for coefficient of first hermite polynomial of density of SDEs. It had an R-Squared of .99964. This is not so perfectly precise as the R-Squared for the previous graph so you can see a little bit of red at the end of green lines as overlaid lines differ in length with very small amount.

Friends, I have generated a few graphs of comparison between true Hermite representation of SDE density and SDE density whose hermite coefficients were generated from regression using original variables/parameters (as opposed to hermite or Legendre based regression). There were 127 combinations of seven original variables and we used a series with sixth power for each variable in regression given as

\[\,Y\,=\, a_0\,+\, \sum\limits_{m=1}^{127} \, \,\sum\limits_{l=1}^{6} \, a_{m,l} \, \left( \, X_m \,-\, \tilde{X}_{m} \right)^l \, \] 

where the variable with tilde over it \(  \tilde{X}_{m} \) represents the median value 

If you know form of your series expansion in an analytical problem, you can apply least squared regression to determine its coefficients with great success. We have just used a generic form without any implied knowledge about the true expansion.

This series was simply estimated with least squares. I did not remove any of the 127 possible regressors but they could further be reduced to at least half their number without any significant effect on quality of regression. Total SDE sample used for regression had 5000 SDEs in it. We just discretized a common terminal point T for all SDEs.

function [] = SDEsWithMathAIMain08E8Standard()


%The mean reverting SDE is given as
%dY(t)= kappa (theta - Y(t)) dt + sigma Y(t)^gamma dZ(t)
%Y(0) = y0


%We specify Low and High SDE parameters range below.
kappaL=.25;%Lower range of kappa
kappaH=3;%Upper range of kappa
thetaL=.5;%Lower range of theta
thetaH=2;%Upper range of theta
y0L=.5;%Lower range of y0 
y0H=2;%Higher range of y0
gammaL=.5;%Lower range of gamma
gammaH=1;%Higher range of gamma
sigmaL=.1;
sigmaH=1.0;


%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
%Below five sets of uniform random numbers are sampled in batches of one
%thousand. These random numbers are later mapped on five input parameters
%over the their range previously defined. 
%Sampling is done in batches of thousands since I wanted to first simulate
%one thousand SDEs and later continue to add more batches of thousand SDEs 
%without spoiling the data of earlier batches from random number sequence.
%Since I was doing antithetic sampling, I did it at level of local batches
%of thousand.

seed=83098561;
rng(seed);
MM=5;    %No of Batches of simulation
NN0=1000;%Scenarios in each batch.
NN=MM*NN0;%Number of SDE runs in the training sample. Ideally would be much larger.

for mm=1:MM
    SampleRand(1:NN0/2,1)=rand(NN0/2,1);    
    Rand1((mm-1)*NN0+1:(mm-1)*NN0+NN0/2,1)=SampleRand(1:NN0/2,1);
    Rand1((mm-1)*NN0+NN0/2+1:mm*NN0,1)=1-SampleRand(1:NN0/2,1);%For symmetry, antithetic uniforms are used.


    SampleRand(1:NN0/2,1)=rand(NN0/2,1);    
    Rand2((mm-1)*NN0+1:(mm-1)*NN0+NN0/2,1)=SampleRand(1:NN0/2,1);
    Rand2((mm-1)*NN0+NN0/2+1:mm*NN0,1)=1-SampleRand(1:NN0/2,1);%For symmetry, antithetic uniforms are used.

    SampleRand(1:NN0/2,1)=rand(NN0/2,1);    
    Rand3((mm-1)*NN0+1:(mm-1)*NN0+NN0/2,1)=SampleRand(1:NN0/2,1);
    Rand3((mm-1)*NN0+NN0/2+1:mm*NN0,1)=1-SampleRand(1:NN0/2,1);%For symmetry, antithetic uniforms are used.


    SampleRand(1:NN0/2,1)=rand(NN0/2,1);    
    Rand4((mm-1)*NN0+1:(mm-1)*NN0+NN0/2,1)=SampleRand(1:NN0/2,1);
    Rand4((mm-1)*NN0+NN0/2+1:mm*NN0,1)=1-SampleRand(1:NN0/2,1);%For symmetry, antithetic uniforms are used.


    SampleRand(1:NN0/2,1)=rand(NN0/2,1);    
    Rand5((mm-1)*NN0+1:(mm-1)*NN0+NN0/2,1)=SampleRand(1:NN0/2,1);
    Rand5((mm-1)*NN0+NN0/2+1:mm*NN0,1)=1-SampleRand(1:NN0/2,1);%For symmetry, antithetic uniforms are used.

end


kappaU(1:NN)=kappaL+(kappaH-kappaL).*Rand1(1:NN,1);%Map unifrom random variates on kappa.

thetaU(1:NN)=thetaL+(thetaH-thetaL).*Rand2(1:NN,1);%Map unifrom random variates on theta.
  
y0U(1:NN)=y0L+(y0H-y0L).*Rand3(1:NN,1);%Map unifrom random variates on starting value of the SDE.

gammaU(1:NN)=gammaL+(gammaH-gammaL).*Rand4(1:NN,1);%Map unifrom random variates on gamma.

sigmaU(1:NN)=sigmaL+(sigmaH-sigmaL).*Rand5(1:NN,1);%Taking sigma at mid of upper and lower range.

Exploring Math AI: Choice of Explanatory Regressors In a Least Square Regression

Let us look more closely at the regression equation from previous post.

There were 127 combinations of seven original variables and we used a series with sixth power for each variable in regression given as


\[\,Y\,=\, a_0\,+\, \sum\limits_{m=1}^{127} \, \,\sum\limits_{l=1}^{6} \, a_{m,l} \, \left( \, X_m \,-\, \tilde{X}_{m} \right)^l \, \] 

where the variable with tilde over it \(  \tilde{X}_{m} \) represents the median value.

It is obvious that we could have used mean instead of median, or merely zero origin instead of median to get the same results. This is just like a classic regression, then why does it work so well even in original variables ?

The answer lies in choice of variables that we have selected for regression and they are very different from what we used to choose in classic regression models.

In order to get a proper intuition, we would have to go back to Hermite polynomial series regression again. 

Suppose we want to regress a random variable V on strictly positive random variables X, Y and their products. We would have to make hermite/Legendre series of all of the above variables as

\[\, X(Z_X)\,=\, ah_0 \, + \, ah_1 \, H_1(Z_X) \,+ \, ah_2 \, H_2(Z_X) \,+ \ldots \,+ \, ah_N \, H_N(Z_X) \, \] 
\[\, Y(Z_Y)\,=\, bh_0 \, + \, bh_1 \, H_1(Z_Y) \,+ \, bh_2 \, H_2(Z_Y) \,+ \ldots \,+ \, bh_N \, H_N(Z_Y) \, \]

Hermite Series of Various products would be given as 
\[\, XY(Z_{XY})\,=\, ch_0 \, + \, ch_1 \, H_1(Z_{XY}) \,+ \, ch_2 \, H_2(Z_{XY}) \,+ \ldots \,+ \, ch_N \, H_N(Z_{XY}) \, \] 
\[\, X^2Y(Z_{X^2Y})\,=\, dh_0 \, + \, dh_1 \, H_1(Z_{X^2Y}) \,+ \, dh_2 \, H_2(Z_{X^2Y}) \,+ \ldots \,+ \, dh_N \, H_N(Z_{X^2Y}) \, \] 
\[\, XY^2(Z_{XY^2})\,=\, eh_0 \, + \, eh_1 \, H_1(Z_{XY^2}) \,+ \, eh_2 \, H_2(Z_{XY^2}) \,+ \ldots \,+ \, eh_N \, H_N(Z_{XY^2}) \, \] 

It is easy to see when X and Y random variables are strictly positive and are uncorrelated/independent, then Z_{XY}, Z_{X^2Y}, and Z_{XY^2} are exactly the same and including all three of them would make the regression matrix singular when doing hermite polynomial regression. So from there it can be learnt that we need to include just one product out of above three.

From above I learnt that for a complete span in the space of products of variables as in above case, when there are two variables, we only need to include X, Y and XY in our regression. 2^2-1 total regressors excluding constant of regression.

when there are three original variables, we only need to include V, X, Y, VX, VY, XY, VXY in our regression. 2^3-1 total regressors.

when there are four original variables, we only need to include U, V, X, Y, UV, UX, UY, VX, VY, XY, UVX, UVY, UXY, VXY, UVXY in our regression. 2^4-1 total regressors.

So for N original variables, we will need 2^N-1 regressors.

Not all of these regressors might be needed but many times we would not know in advance. In order to do analytical analysis about which variables to exclude out of 2^N-1 regressors, you can read this post. https://forum.wilmott.com/viewtopic.php?t=99702&start=2520#p884966

In our example of SDE density hermite coefficients, we used seven starting variables, so there were a total of 127 regressors.

This choice of regressors is very different from what was usually used in classic regressions. So sometimes we can just use classic regression models with an optimum choice of regressors to get excellent results.

Please note when a variable is not strictly positive and can be both positive and negative, the above analysis would need to be modified with some additional products in the regression.

Dynamic Coefficients Hermite Polynomial Series Models For Time Series Evolution of Random Variables

 In this brief note we want to discuss hermite polynomial series representation of random variables where coefficients of hermite polynomials are also represented by another hermite polynomial series for each coefficient.

We give some example of some practical dynamic hermite series coefficients models below

\[\, X(Z,Z_0,Z_1,Z_2,Z_3)\,=\, \left(a_{00} \,+\,a_{01}\,Z_0 \right)\,+ \, \left( a_{10} \,+\, a_{11}\, Z_1 \right) \, Z\, + \, \left( a_{20} \,+\, a_{21} \, Z_2 \right) \,\, \left( Z^2\,-\,1\,\right) \\ + \, \left(a_{30} \,+\,a_{31}\,Z_3\right) \, \left( Z^3\,-\,3 \,Z\,\right)    \] 

The above model can also be written as

\[\, X(Z,Z_0,Z_1,Z_2,Z_3)\,=\, a_{0}(Z_0)\, + \, \, a_{1}(Z_1)\, \, Z\, + \, \, a_{2}(Z_2)\,\, \left( Z^2\,-\,1\,\right) + \, \, a_{3}(Z_3)\, \, \left( Z^3\,-\,3 \,Z\,\right)    \] 
where
\[\, a_{0}(Z_0)\, =\, \left(a_{00} \,+\,a_{01}\,Z_0 \right)\,\]
\[\, a_{1}(Z_1)\, =\, \left(a_{10} \,+\,a_{11}\,Z_1 \right)\,\]
\[\, a_{2}(Z_2)\, =\, \left(a_{20} \,+\,a_{21}\,Z_2 \right)\,\]
\[\, a_{3}(Z_3)\, =\, \left(a_{30} \,+\,a_{31}\,Z_3 \right)\,\]

We would mostly need to give dynamics to coefficients of only first four polynomials in the hermite series.  All of these \(Z_0\, \,Z_1, \, Z_2,\,Z_3 \) would not be correlated with each other but could be correlated with \(\bar{Z}_0\, \,\bar{Z}_1, \, \bar{Z}_2,\, \bar{Z}_3 \) which are dynamic coefficients of hermite series of another random variable.


Estimation of the coefficients in the Dynamic Coefficients Hermite Series Model

The above model has eight coefficients and these coefficients of the model can easily be estimated by some numerical method that uses multidimensional Newton to fit first eight moments of the data to model. I will be posting this Newton program in next few days. Again knowledge of eight moments of data would be required to find the eight coefficients in the model.


Estimation of the Present State of coefficients in the Dynamic Coefficients Hermite Series Model

Even after estimation of all coefficients in the model, we do not perfectly know the values of \( a_0(Z_0), \, a_1(Z_1),\, a_2(Z_2), \, a_3(Z_3) \) since they depend upon their respective Z's. And we also have to know that one observation (one degree of freedom) is not enough to pin down all these four Z's. However, we can use a sliding window of anywhere between  4 - 10 or higher number of time intervals to estimate first four local moments of the time series. We can use these local values of moments to estimate the model

\[\, X(Z)\,=\, a_{0}\, + \, \, a_{1}\, \, Z\, + \, \, a_{2}\,\, \left( Z^2\,-\,1\,\right) + \, \, a_{3}\, \, \left( Z^3\,-\,3 \,Z\,\right)    \] 

We would have to use Newton inversion on each step to find the coefficients  \( a_0, \, a_1,\, a_2, \, a_3 \) from the above equation fitted to local data moments and use the relationships
\[\, a_{0}\, =\, \left(a_{00} \,+\,a_{01}\,Z_0 \right)\,\]
\[\, a_{1}\, =\, \left(a_{10} \,+\,a_{11}\,Z_1 \right)\,\]
\[\, a_{2}\, =\, \left(a_{20} \,+\,a_{21}\,Z_2 \right)\,\]
\[\, a_{3}\, =\, \left(a_{30} \,+\,a_{31}\,Z_3 \right)\,\]

to find the values of \(Z_0, \, Z_1,\, Z_2, \, Z_3 \)  which can be used to calculate their correlations with dynamic coefficients of some other random variable through values of  \(\bar{Z}_0\, \,\bar{Z}_1, \, \bar{Z}_2,\, \bar{Z}_3 \) of dynamic coefficients of the other random variable. With each new time interval, the sliding window will move ahead by one time interval and values of first four local moments would change and that would result in update values of  \( a_0, \, a_1,\, a_2, \, a_3 \) found from inversion of new first four moments giving us new updated values of \(Z_0, \, Z_1,\, Z_2, \, Z_3 \) for the new interval.

Explanation of the Model and its Usefulness

We do one global optimization on data to learn the parameters. And then we do local optimizations on local data to find the values of \(Z_0, \, Z_1,\, Z_2, \, Z_3 \). The first four hermite polynomials terms are highly correlated with first four moments of the data respectively. So knowledge of  \( a_0, \, a_1,\, a_2, \, a_3 \) found from local data would tell us about local values of mean, variance, skew and kurtosis. And values of  \(Z_0, \, Z_1,\, Z_2, \, Z_3 \) would tell us how local values of mean, variance, skew and kurtosis have changed in terms of their respective standardized indicators  (standard normal values) based on the range found in global data. 

The knowledge of local values and the standard deviations from averages for mean, variance, skew and kurtosis would be extremely helpful in vanilla stocks trading strategies and also in option pricing trading strategies. We will explore these uses of the model when we apply them to real stocks and option pricing data next year.
